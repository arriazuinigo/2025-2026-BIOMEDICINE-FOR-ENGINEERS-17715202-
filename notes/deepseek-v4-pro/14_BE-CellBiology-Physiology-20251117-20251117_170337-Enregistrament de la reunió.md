## Summary
This session opened Unit 5 with an overview of the human body systems, then covered the integumentary system and the locomotor system in detail. The professor explained how skin functions as a physical and chemical barrier, participates in thermoregulation and sensation, and how it is organised into epidermis, dermis, and the underlying hypodermis. The second half focused on the skeletal system: bone functions, bone classification, bone tissue structure, growth, and remodelling. It closed with the organisation of skeletal muscle from cells to whole muscles, including the sarcomere.

- The integumentary system is much more than skin: it is a barrier, a blood reservoir, a thermoregulatory surface, a vitamin D synthesis site, and a sensory organ.
- Skin proper consists of two layers: epidermis (avascular epithelial barrier) and dermis (vascular connective tissue); the hypodermis lies below and is mostly adipocytes.
- Bones are living, highly vascularised connective tissue with mineralised extracellular matrix, not inert structures.
- Bone is constantly remodelled by osteoblasts and osteoclasts, which also helps regulate blood calcium.
- The adult skeleton has 206 bones, with shape-based types (long, flat, short, irregular) and location/formation-based types (sesamoid, sutural).
- Skeletal muscle cells are multinucleated and striated, organised into fascicles; tendons are connective tissue extensions that attach muscle to bone.

## Key concepts

- **Integumentary system** – the body system formed mainly by skin, plus associated glands, hair, and nails [00:01:01].
- **Locomotor system** – the combination of the skeletal system (bones, cartilage, articulations) and the muscular system (skeletal muscles, tendons) [00:01:01].
- **Epidermis** – the most superficial skin layer; a stratified epithelium with no blood vessels [00:14:55].
- **Dermis** – the connective tissue layer below the epidermis, containing blood vessels, collagen, and glands [00:16:52].
- **Hypodermis** – the layer below the skin proper, formed mainly by adipocytes; contains hair bulbs and sweat glands; not considered part of the skin [00:20:59].
- **Osteon** – the basic structural and functional unit of compact bone, organised as concentric rings around a central blood-vessel channel [00:43:47].
- **Osteoblast / osteocyte / osteoclast** – cells that respectively form bone matrix, maintain bone while trapped in it, and resorb/degrade bone [00:49:23].
- **Sarcomere** – the functional contractile unit of a muscle cell, made of highly organised thick and thin filaments [00:54:57].

## Detailed notes

### Unit 5 overview: the body systems
The unit covers the systems that coordinate or sustain the body [00:00:00].

- **Integumentary system**: skin, associated glands, hair, nails [00:01:01].
- **Locomotor system**: skeleton (bones, cartilage, articulations) plus skeletal muscles and tendons [00:01:01].
- **Cardiovascular system**: heart, arteries, veins; distributes blood and components throughout the body [00:02:20].
- **Lymphatic and immune system**: lymph drainage from tissues back to blood, immune-cell formation in bone marrow, and body protection [00:02:20].
- **Respiratory system**: conducts air to the lungs; eliminates carbon dioxide [00:03:41].
- **Digestive system**: obtains most nutrients for cells [00:03:41].
- **Urinary system**: eliminates waste; main role of the kidney [00:04:48].
- **Reproductive system**: male and female differences; hormonal regulation of gamete production (oocytes, spermatozoids) [00:04:48].
- **Endocrine system**: glands producing hormones (thyroid, hypophysis); coordinates body systems via hormones [00:05:57].
- **Nervous system**: brain and central nervous system, plus peripheral nervous system; coordinates body function [00:07:06].

### Integumentary system: functions
The skin is about more than covering the body [00:08:08].

- **Physical protection**: a physical barrier separating the inside of the body from the environment [00:08:08].
- **Chemical/immune protection**: skin surface secretions help block pathogen entry; the barrier is also chemical, not only physical [00:09:10].
- **Prevents dehydration**: reduces strong evaporation of internal liquids to the environment [00:09:10].
- **UV protection**: reduces strong ultraviolet irradiation reaching internal structures [00:09:40].
- **Secretion**: sweat from sudoriferous glands and secretions from sebaceous glands are produced and released to the surface [00:10:23].
- **Absorption**: some substances contacting skin can enter the body depending on chemical composition. Lipid-soluble substances are more permeable, gases can cross, and some drugs or vitamins can enter through the skin depending on their composition [00:10:23].
- **Blood reservoir**: about 10% of total blood flow goes through the skin; this blood can be mobilised when needed [00:11:55].
- **Thermoregulation**: regulating blood flow through skin changes heat exchange with the environment. Sweating also cools the body because evaporation from the skin surface removes energy as heat [00:11:55].
- **Vitamin D synthesis**: a vitamin D precursor requires ultraviolet irradiation to become active; this transformation happens in the skin [00:13:04].
- **Sensation**: receptors in the skin detect touch, vibration, pressure, temperature (thermoreceptors), and pain, and send this information to coordination systems such as the nervous system [00:14:11].

### Skin structure: epidermis, dermis, hypodermis
Skin covers almost 100% of the body surface [00:14:55].

**Epidermis**
- The most superficial layer; formed by epithelial tissue [00:14:55].
- It is a stratified epithelium, meaning several layers of cells [00:14:55].
- Cells in the deeper internal layer proliferate; newly formed cells differentiate and eventually die as they progress toward the surface [00:15:20].
- As an epithelium, the epidermis has no blood vessels; it is avascular [00:15:50].
- Its role is to form the barrier between the inner body and the outside [00:16:20].

**Dermis**
- Below the epidermis; a connective tissue [00:16:52].
- Contains few cells and abundant extracellular matrix, including collagen [00:16:52].
- Together, dermis and epidermis form the “skin proper” [00:16:52].
- Blood vessels run through the dermis; the epidermis receives nutrients by diffusion from the dermis because the two layers are adjacent [00:17:30].
- Contains sebaceous glands. In most of the body, sebaceous glands are associated with hair follicles and secrete into them; in hairless areas such as the lips, they secrete directly onto the skin surface [00:18:47].
- Hair follicles are invaginations of the epidermis that follow the hair down to the bulb and back to the surface [00:20:59].

**Hypodermis**
- The layer below the skin; not considered part of the skin proper [00:20:59].
- Contains the hair bulbs (origin of hair) [00:19:55].
- Contains sweat glands [00:22:00].
- The rest is mainly adipocytes, cells specialised in storing lipids; so the hypodermis is a fatty connective tissue [00:22:00].

### Skeletal system: functions
The skeletal system includes bones, cartilage, and articulations [00:23:47].

- **Support**: structural support for the body [00:23:47].
- **Protection**: protects internal organs; examples are the brain inside the skull and the lungs and heart inside the thoracic cage [00:23:47].
- **Movement**: bones are points of attachment for muscles [00:23:47].
- **Mineral homeostasis**: helps regulate calcium and phosphorus concentrations in the blood/internal medium [00:23:47].
- **Blood cell production**: cavities inside bones contain red bone marrow, which continuously produces blood cells [00:24:40].
- **Energy storage**: yellow bone marrow inside bone cavities is formed by adipocytes, storing lipids, especially triglycerides, as an energy reservoir [00:25:00].

### Muscular system: role in locomotion
- Skeletal muscles produce body movement and stabilise positions; they are the muscles of the locomotor system [00:25:47].
- Cardiac muscle pumps blood and is studied with the cardiovascular system [00:25:47].
- Smooth muscle participates in other systems by mobilising substances, for example blood in vessels and food in the intestines [00:26:30].

### Bone classification
Bones can be classified by shape, and some by how or where they form [00:26:53].

**By shape**
- **Long bones**: one dimension is much longer than the other two; the typical long shape [00:28:00].
- **Flat bones**: high surface area and small thickness; example: bones of the cranium [00:28:19].
- **Short bones**: small bones with all three axes similar in length, roughly cuboid; example: carpal bones of the wrist [00:28:45].
- **Irregular bones**: bones without a simple reproducible shape, with many surface irregularities; example: vertebrae and some facial bones [00:29:51].

**By formation/location**
- **Sesamoid bones**: form inside tendons. The patella is a sesamoid bone and is present in almost all individuals. The number and position of sesamoid bones vary between people [00:30:55].
- **Sutural bones**: small bones found in the suture between two adjacent skull bones; they also vary in number and position between individuals [00:32:02].

**Adult skeleton numbers**
- 206 total bones [00:32:40].
- 80 axial skeleton bones and 126 appendicular skeleton bones [00:33:03].
- 22 skull bones; 26 vertebral column bones in adults because some vertebrae fuse: 5 sacral vertebrae form one bone and 4 coccygeal vertebrae form one [00:33:30].
- Thorax: ribs plus sternum = 25 bones [00:34:00].
- Hyoid: a special single bone in the neck, surrounded by muscle and not articulated with any other bone [00:34:20].
- Auditory ossicles in the ear are also part of the axial skeleton [00:34:52].
- Upper extremities: 60 bones total, including humerus, radius, ulna, carpals (18 per side), metacarpals (5) and phalanges (14) [00:34:52].
- Lower extremities: similar distribution in legs and feet; pelvic girdle included [00:35:56].
- The two patellae are the two sesamoid bones included in the 206; additional sutural and sesamoid bones vary by individual [00:35:56].

### Bone tissue structure
- Bones are living tissue, not inert rigid material [00:37:10].
- Bone is a connective tissue whose extracellular matrix is mineralised with a high content of calcium phosphate; it also contains proteins such as collagen [00:37:10].
- The outer portion of a bone is dense **compact bone**; the inner portion is less dense **cancellous/spongy bone**, with spaces between mineralised structures [00:38:10].
- The spaces and the central cavity of long bones house bone marrow [00:38:10].

**Long bone regions**
- **Diaphysis**: the central shaft [00:39:14].
- **Epiphysis**: the ends [00:39:14].
- **Metaphysis**: the zone between diaphysis and epiphysis [00:39:14].
- The blood supply of the epiphysis is independent from that of the diaphysis; a line separates the two irrigation systems [00:39:50].
- Bone is highly vascularised. Bone surgery can bleed significantly, and sometimes patients need blood transfusions [00:40:19].
- Bone is also well innervated; bone lesions are painful because of abundant nerve endings [00:41:30].
- In adults, the central diaphysis cavity contains mostly yellow marrow; the epiphysis contains red bone marrow [00:42:21].

**Compact and cancellous bone**
- Compact bone lies on the surface of all bones; its thickness varies [00:43:47].
- The basic structural/functional unit of compact bone is the **osteon**: concentric rings of bone organised around a central channel containing blood vessels [00:43:47].
- Osteocytes trapped in bone have cell-membrane extensions that allow contact with surrounding cells, the central channel, or the bone surface, so they can receive nutrients and signals [00:44:00].
- Cancellous bone has spaces between mineralised structures; yellow marrow may occupy these spaces [00:44:52].

### Bone growth, separate blood supplies, and remodelling
- During growth in childhood and adolescence, a long bone has a **primary ossification centre** in the diaphysis and a **secondary ossification centre** in the epiphysis [00:46:08].
- The two centres remain separated by a cartilage plate during growth [00:46:30].
- Cartilage is a connective tissue that does not allow blood vessels to pass through it [00:47:09].
- This separation is visible on X-rays of children as a non-rigid cartilage line; each bone closes at a particular age, the last ones in adulthood [00:47:09].
- When the epiphysis and diaphysis fuse after growth, the bone becomes one continuous structure, but the separate blood arrival patterns are maintained [00:48:12].
- Bone is dynamic throughout life: bone is constantly formed and degraded [00:49:00].
- **Osteoblasts** synthesise the mineralised matrix and, after becoming trapped, become **osteocytes** [00:49:23].
- **Osteoclasts** degrade bone [00:49:40].
- If osteoblast and osteoclast activity are similar, net bone growth is zero. During adolescence osteoblast activity predominates and bone forms; in ageing osteoclast activity may predominate, causing net bone loss [00:50:00].
- Bone formation removes calcium from blood; bone degradation releases calcium into blood. Therefore, balancing bone formation and resorption helps regulate blood calcium levels [00:50:38].

### Skeletal muscle organisation
- There are about 700 muscles in the human body [00:51:39].
- Muscle cells are organised into bundles called **fascicles**; groups of fascicles form a whole muscle [00:51:39].
- Connective tissue surrounds each individual muscle cell, each fascicle, and the entire muscle [00:52:49].
- At the ends of the muscle, this connective tissue continues beyond the muscle cells and attaches to bone; that structure is the **tendon** [00:52:49].

### Muscle cell structure: introduction
- Skeletal muscle cells are **multinucleated** because several precursor cells fuse to form a single larger cell [00:53:56].
- They have a striated appearance because of the internal organisation of proteins [00:54:00].
- Skeletal muscle cells require nerve arrival (innervation) to contract [00:54:57].
- Inside the cell, two major protein types form thick and thin filaments [00:54:57].
- These filaments are organised so precisely that they create functional units called **sarcomeres**, the contractile units of muscle [00:55:20].
- The next session will explain how the sarcomere organisation enables the multinucleated cell to change shape during contraction [00:56:59].

## Self-test

1. Why can skin be described as both a physical and a chemical immune barrier?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The epidermis/cells form a physical barrier separating the internal body from the outside. Chemical protection comes from secretions on the skin surface that help impede pathogen entry, so the barrier is both physical and chemical.
</details>

2. How does blood flow through the skin contribute to thermoregulation?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Skin acts as a blood reservoir, with about 10% of total blood flow passing through it. Regulating this blood flow changes how much heat is exchanged with the environment. Sweating adds further cooling because evaporation of sweat from the skin surface removes heat energy.
</details>

3. Which layers form the skin proper, and what lies below them?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The skin proper is formed by the epidermis (superficial avascular stratified epithelium) and the dermis (underlying vascular connective tissue). Below them is the hypodermis, which is not part of the skin proper and is mainly composed of adipocytes, while containing hair bulbs and sweat glands.
</details>

4. How does the epidermis receive nutrients if it has no blood vessels?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Nutrients arrive in the blood vessels of the dermis. Because the dermis and epidermis are directly adjacent, nutrients reach the epidermis by diffusion over the short distance between the two layers.
</details>

5. Why are sesamoid and sutural bones not classified as long, flat, short, or irregular?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
They are classified instead by how or where they form: sesamoid bones form inside tendons (e.g., patella), and sutural bones form within the sutures between adjacent skull bones. Their number and position also vary between individuals.
</details>

6. A child’s long-bone X-ray shows a cartilage line between the epiphysis and diaphysis. What does this indicate, and what changes in adulthood?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It indicates the bone is still growing: a cartilage growth plate separates the primary ossification centre in the diaphysis from the secondary ossification centre in the epiphysis. Cartilage is avascular, so blood supplies are separate. At the end of growth the epiphysis and diaphysis fuse into one continuous bone, but the separate blood-arrival patterns remain.
</details>

7. What would happen to bone mass and blood calcium if osteoclast activity exceeded osteoblast activity?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Bone degradation would exceed bone formation, causing net loss of bone mass. Degrading bone releases calcium into the bloodstream, so blood calcium would tend to increase.
</details>

8. Explain how the structure of an osteon keeps trapped osteocytes alive in compact bone.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
An osteon is formed by concentric bone layers around a central channel containing blood vessels. Osteocytes trapped in the bone extend cell-membrane processes to contact surrounding cells, the central channel, or the bone surface, allowing them to receive nutrients and information.
</details>

9. How is a tendon related to the connective tissue coverings of a skeletal muscle?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Connective tissue surrounds each muscle cell, each fascicle, and the whole muscle. At the muscle ends, this connective tissue continues beyond the muscle cells and attaches to bone; this continuation is the tendon.
</details>

10. Why are skeletal muscle cells described as multinucleated and striated?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
They are multinucleated because several precursor cells fuse to form one larger cell. They appear striated because of the highly ordered internal arrangement of the thick and thin protein filaments that form sarcomeres.
</details>
