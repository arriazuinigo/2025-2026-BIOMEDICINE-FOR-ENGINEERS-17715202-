## Summary

This session introduced the molecular biology half of the course (Units 1–3), starting with a broad overview of the human genome. The professor covered why cells are the fundamental unit of life, how DNA stores genetic information, how 2 metres of DNA is packed into chromosomes inside the nucleus, the organisation of genes and intergenic regions, and the central dogma (DNA → RNA → protein). She then introduced nucleic acids: nucleotide structure, the differences between DNA and RNA, base pairing, and why single-stranded RNA folds into a three-dimensional shape. The session ended with a short PyMOL demonstration to visualise an RNA structure.

Key takeaways:

- Cells are the fundamental unit of life, and all organisms share the same genetic code carried by DNA.
- The human genome is approximately 3.2 × 10⁹ nucleotides, split into 23 chromosome pairs (46 chromosomes) and compacted with proteins as chromatin.
- Intergenic regions are not “junk DNA”; they contain information controlling when and how genes are expressed.
- The central dogma: 60,000 genes can be transcribed into RNA, but only about 20,000 of those RNA molecules are translated into proteins.
- Cells differ from each other mainly because they express different sets of proteins, and gene expression is highly dynamic and tightly regulated.
- DNA and RNA are polynucleotides with a sugar-phosphate backbone; DNA is double-stranded with T, RNA is single-stranded with U, and RNA folds according to A–U and C–G base pairing.

## Detailed notes

### 1. Cells as the fundamental unit of life and DNA as the genetic material [00:04:13–00:07:24]

- There are more than 100 million species of living organisms, all made of cells; some are unicellular, others are made of many cells [00:04:13].
- Cells are membrane-enclosed units filled with concentrated aqueous solution and many chemicals [00:05:15].
- Cells vary greatly in shape and size, from micrometres to millimetres [00:05:15].
- Despite differences, cells contain the same broad types of molecules: DNA, RNA, proteins, ions, small chemicals, peptides, and carbohydrates [00:06:20].
- Genetic information needed for survival and reproduction is carried in DNA, and the genetic code is the same across organisms [00:06:20].
- Life depends on the cell’s ability to store, retrieve, and translate the genetic information in DNA, and this information is passed from cell to daughter cell and between generations [00:07:24].

### 2. The human genome: size, sequence, and packaging [00:08:27–00:11:57]

- DNA is made of nucleotides: thymine (red), cytosine (blue), adenine (yellow), and guanine (green) [00:08:27].
- Human DNA contains 3.2 × 10⁹ nucleotides [00:08:27].
- The genome can be represented simply as a sequence of A, C, T, G; the professor called this the **informatic genome** [00:09:32].
- The total human DNA is about 2 metres long, but the nucleus is only 5–8 μm in diameter [00:09:38].
- To fit into the nucleus, the DNA is first split into 23 chromosomes (two sets: maternal and paternal) and then packed with proteins via electrostatic interactions [00:09:38].
- This DNA–protein complex is called **chromatin** [00:10:50].
- The packing is essential because the DNA must be compacted enough to fit within the nucleus [00:10:50].

### 3. Chromosomes, karyotype, and chromatin organisation [00:11:57–00:14:45]

- All human cells, except germ cells, contain two copies of each chromosome, giving 46 chromosomes in total [00:11:57].
- Maternal and paternal copies of each chromosome are called **homologous chromosomes** [00:11:57].
- Sex chromosomes are an exception: males have X and Y, females have two X chromosomes [00:11:57].
- The complete set of 46 chromosomes is the **human karyotype** [00:11:57].
- Under the microscope, chromosomes show dark and light bands [00:12:50].
  - Dark regions: highly packed DNA with many proteins, low gene density, less accessible [00:13:00].
  - Light regions: less packed DNA, more accessible, high gene density; genes are found mainly here [00:13:00].
- The accessibility of light regions allows processes such as transcription to start [00:13:30].

### 4. Genome definition and comparison of eukaryotic and prokaryotic genomes [00:14:45–00:19:10]

- The genome is the entire sequence of nucleotides (A, T, C, G) in an organism’s DNA, containing the total genetic information [00:15:47].
- Most organisms have DNA genomes, but some viruses have RNA genomes [00:15:47].
- The complete genomic sequence has been determined for thousands of organisms, from *Escherichia coli* to humans, mammals, rodents, and plants; sequencing costs keep falling [00:14:45].
- Genome size varies widely: viruses and bacteria have small genomes; animals, plants, mammals, and humans have large genomes [00:16:00].
- **Eukaryotic genome**:
  - Main DNA is in the nucleus [00:16:59].
  - Some DNA is in the cytoplasm, but it is not considered part of the genome [00:16:59].
  - DNA is linear [00:18:04].
  - Replication can start from many origins [00:18:04].
- **Prokaryotic genome**:
  - DNA is in the cytoplasm and is circular [00:16:59].
  - It is not packed with proteins to the same degree as eukaryotic DNA, although some proteins do interact with the bacterial chromosome [00:17:20].
  - There is an additional smaller circular DNA in the cytoplasm, the **plasmid DNA**, which can carry genes such as antibiotic resistance genes [00:18:04].
  - Replication starts from a single origin [00:19:10].
- In both cases, DNA is double-stranded: one strand plus its complementary strand [00:19:10].

### 5. Genes, intergenic regions, and chromosome anatomy [00:19:10–00:24:17]

- **Gene**: a segment of DNA, thousands of nucleotides long, that contains the instructions for making an RNA molecule; from some RNAs, a protein can be made [00:20:11].
- The professor initially stated the human genome contains 60,000 genes [00:20:11].
  - Later, when comparing species, she gave the number 22,000 genes for humans [00:23:10].
  - These statements appear inconsistent; in context, the 60,000 may refer to all RNA-producing segments of DNA, while only about 20,000 are translated into protein. The 22,000 figure likely refers to protein-coding genes in a species comparison. (This is a note by the student, not stated explicitly by the professor.)
- **Intergenic regions**: DNA between genes; once considered “junk DNA”, but now known to contain information controlling when and how genes are expressed [00:21:00].
- A chromosome has a **P arm**, a **centromere**, and a **Q arm**, plus visible bands [00:22:01].
- A gene can be located by specifying the arm (P or Q) and the band number [00:23:10].
- Gene number is only roughly correlated with species complexity [00:23:55]:
  - Grapes: ~30,000 genes
  - Humans: ~22,000 genes
  - Chicken: ~16,000 genes
- Chromosome number and total genome size are **not** well correlated with complexity [00:24:17].
- In bacteria, genes are packed closely together; in humans, genes are separated by large intergenic regions, and the human genome has more intergenic than genic regions [00:24:17].

### 6. Central dogma and protein diversity [00:24:17–00:28:31]

- The **central dogma of molecular biology** describes the flow of genetic information: DNA → RNA → protein [00:24:17].
- DNA contains instructions to make RNA; RNA contains the information to make a protein [00:24:17].
- Proteins have a three-dimensional structure and perform most functions inside cells; some RNA molecules also perform functions [00:25:21].
- Of the 60,000 genes that can be transcribed into RNA:
  - 20,000 RNA molecules are translated into proteins [00:25:21].
  - 40,000 RNA molecules are not translated; they function as non-coding RNAs and also regulate gene expression and translation [00:26:30].
- Proteins are made of 20 different amino acids; different sequences and combinations produce different three-dimensional structures and therefore different functions [00:27:50].
- Similar protein shapes tend to produce similar functions; different shapes produce different functions [00:28:31].
- What makes one cell different from another is the set of proteins it contains [00:28:31].
- Proteins must be made at the proper time and place [00:29:34].

### 7. Gene expression and its regulation [00:28:31–00:40:40]

- **Gene expression** is the process of turning on a gene to produce RNA and, ultimately, protein [00:29:34].
- Cells do not express all 20,000 proteins at once; they regulate expression according to cell type, environment, and needs [00:29:34].
- The large intergenic regions contain the information for when to express a gene and produce the corresponding protein [00:30:00].
- Control of gene expression is extremely complex, and malfunctions can lead to diseases, including cancer [00:30:36].
- Regulation can occur at multiple steps [00:31:00]:
  - Transcription
  - RNA processing
  - RNA transport to the cytoplasm
  - Translation
  - Post-translational modification
- Each step can be stopped or activated to control protein production [00:32:00].
- Different tissues are made of cells with different shapes and functions because they express different sets of proteins [00:32:00].
- Important: a cell type does not express only one protein; it expresses thousands of proteins, with some shared among cell types and some specific [00:35:55].
- The proteome is dynamic; the set of expressed proteins changes with [00:37:31]:
  - Developmental stage (young vs old)
  - Disease state
  - Environment (e.g., living in another country, UV exposure)
  - Exercise or sport
  - Diet
- This environmental influence on gene expression is connected to **epigenetics** [00:38:32].
- During development, regulator proteins bind DNA to control which proteins are produced and thereby differentiate cells into different tissues [00:39:33].

### 8. Nucleic acids: nucleotide structure and DNA vs RNA [00:40:40–00:46:20]

- Nucleic acids are naturally occurring chemical compounds that carry information; the two types are DNA and RNA [00:40:40].
- A nucleotide consists of [00:41:43]:
  - A phosphate group (negatively charged)
  - A sugar ring
  - A nitrogenous base
- The phosphate group is always the same; the sugar differs between RNA and DNA [00:42:10].
- Bases found in DNA: adenine (A), cytosine (C), thymine (T), guanine (G) [00:42:45].
- Bases found in RNA: adenine (A), cytosine (C), uracil (U), guanine (G) [00:42:45].
- Thymine and uracil are chemically very similar; the main difference is that thymine has an extra methyl group [00:42:45].
- Nucleotides are joined by **phosphodiester bonds**, forming a repeating sugar-phosphate backbone with variable bases [00:43:49].
- A chain of nucleotides is a **polynucleotide chain** [00:43:49].
- RNA is single-stranded; DNA is double-stranded [00:43:49].
- In DNA, the bases are not exposed because they interact with the bases of the complementary strand [00:45:15].
- Sequences are written by convention in the **5′ to 3′** direction; this polarity is important [00:45:15].
- DNA: contains thymine, is double-stranded, and is usually very long [00:46:20].
- RNA: contains uracil, is single-stranded, and is usually shorter [00:46:20].

### 9. RNA folding and base pairing [00:46:20–00:49:39]

- Because RNA is single-stranded, its bases are exposed and have many potential hydrogen bonds that are not satisfied [00:47:23].
- To satisfy this hydrogen-bonding potential, RNA molecules fold into complex three-dimensional structures [00:47:23].
- Folding brings complementary bases together to form hydrogen bonds [00:48:00].
- Base-pairing rules are strict [00:48:31]:
  - C pairs with G
  - A pairs with U in RNA (with T in DNA)
- There are different types of RNA [00:49:00]:
  - Messenger RNAs (mRNAs) — can be translated into proteins
  - Transfer RNAs (tRNAs)
  - Ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs)
  - Non-coding RNAs
  - Small and long RNAs
- The sequence of an mRNA determines the sequence of the corresponding protein [00:49:39].
- Because folded RNA has a three-dimensional shape, it can perform functions inside the cell [00:49:39].

### 10. PyMOL visualisation of RNA [00:49:39–00:59:58]

- The professor asked students to install **PyMOL**, a tool for visualising macromolecules [00:50:32].
- Basic procedure: `Fetch 5TPY` to download an RNA structure, then `Display > Sequence` to view the sequence [00:52:00][00:55:00].
- The displayed RNA molecule has:
  - An orange backbone made of alternating sugar and phosphate groups [00:55:00].
  - Bases pointing outward [00:56:06].
- In DNA, these bases would be paired with complementary bases on the opposite strand; in RNA they are exposed and the molecule folds to maximise base-pairing interactions [00:56:06].
- Only A–U and C–G interactions fit the hydrogen-bonding pattern [00:57:09].
- PyMOL visualisation modes shown [00:58:19]:

  - Sticks: shows the exact chemical structure
  - Spheres: space-filling representation of atoms
- The purpose was to give a mental image of RNA as a real three-dimensional molecule, not just a sequence [01:00:58].

## Self-test

1. How does 2 metres of DNA fit into a nucleus that is only 5–8 μm in diameter?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The DNA is split into 23 chromosomes, which are then packed with proteins via electrostatic interactions to form chromatin. This compaction allows the long DNA molecule to fit inside the nucleus.
</details>

2. Why are genes more likely to be found in the light-staining bands of chromosomes than in the dark bands?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Light bands represent less tightly packed, more accessible DNA with high gene density. Dark bands are highly compacted and less accessible, so they have low gene density.
</details>

3. Distinguish the terms genome, chromosome, chromatin, and gene as used in the session.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
- Genome: the entire DNA sequence of an organism.
- Chromosome: one of the separate, packaged DNA molecules into which the genome is divided.
- Chromatin: the complex of DNA and proteins that compacts DNA within the nucleus.
- Gene: a segment of DNA containing instructions to make an RNA molecule.
</details>

4. Why were intergenic regions once called “junk DNA”, and why is that view incorrect?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
They were called junk DNA because they do not contain protein-coding genes. However, they contain important regulatory information that controls when and how genes are expressed, so they are not junk.
</details>

5. Describe the central dogma and the numbers the professor gave for RNA and protein production.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The central dogma is DNA → RNA → protein. The professor said 60,000 genes can be transcribed into RNA; about 20,000 of those RNAs are translated into proteins, and the remaining 40,000 are non-coding RNAs that perform regulatory or other functions. She later gave 22,000 as the human gene count in a species comparison, so the two figures are inconsistent in the transcript.
</details>

6. Compare eukaryotic and prokaryotic genomes in terms of location, shape, and replication origins.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Eukaryotic genomes: linear DNA located in the nucleus, with some DNA in the cytoplasm not considered part of the genome; replication starts from many origins. Prokaryotic genomes: circular DNA located in the cytoplasm, often with a small circular plasmid; replication starts from a single origin.
</details>

7. What are the main chemical differences between DNA and RNA?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
They differ in the sugar component and in one base: DNA contains thymine (T) while RNA contains uracil (U). Thymine has an extra methyl group compared with uracil. DNA is also double-stranded and generally longer, whereas RNA is single-stranded and usually shorter.
</details>

8. Why does a single-stranded RNA molecule fold, and what rules govern base pairing?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
RNA folds because its exposed bases have unsatisfied hydrogen-bond potential. Folding brings complementary bases together to maximise hydrogen bonding. Base pairing is specific: C pairs with G, and A pairs with U in RNA (A pairs with T in DNA).
</details>

9. If an RNA fragment has the sequence 5′-AUCG-3′, write the complementary RNA sequence in the 5′ to 3′ direction.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
5′-CGAU-3′. The antiparallel complement is 3′-UAGC-5′, which written 5′ to 3′ is 5′-CGAU-3′.
</details>

10. All cells in an organism contain the same genome. Why do muscle cells and liver cells look and function differently?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
They express different sets of proteins, and proteins determine cell structure and function. Gene expression is regulated differently in each cell type, so each produces a specific combination of proteins.
</details>

11. What is the “informatic genome”?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It is the representation of the 3.2 × 10⁹ nucleotides of the human genome as a sequence of letters A, C, T, and G, rather than as chemical structures.
</details>

12. In the PyMOL exercise, what did the RNA structure show about its backbone and bases, and why does the molecule adopt that shape?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The RNA structure showed a sugar-phosphate backbone with bases pointing outward. Because there is no complementary strand, the exposed bases form hydrogen bonds with complementary bases elsewhere in the same molecule (A–U and C–G), causing the RNA to fold into a three-dimensional shape.
</details>
