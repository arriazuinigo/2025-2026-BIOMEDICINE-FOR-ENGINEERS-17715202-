## Summary
This session finished Unit 4 with histology and organography. It covered how cells organise into tissues, the role of the extracellular matrix and cell junctions, the four basic tissue types (epithelial, connective, muscle, nervous), and how tissues combine into organs. The professor then explained the histology techniques used to visualise tissues (biopsy, fixation, staining with haematoxylin and eosin) and introduced Task 1, which asks students to study the tissue organisation of one selected organ.

- A **tissue** is a community of similar cells working together; cells are held together by direct cell-to-cell junctions or by attachment to the **extracellular matrix**.
- There are **four basic tissue types**: epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous.
- Epithelial tissue forms barriers and is classified by **cell shape** (squamous/cuboidal/columnar) and **number of layers** (simple/stratified/pseudostratified), and can form exocrine/endocrine glands.
- Connective tissue is defined by **few cells and abundant extracellular matrix**, which is why bone, cartilage and even blood belong to this group.
- Organs are structures made of **two or more tissue types**; routine histology uses fixation and staining, typically **haematoxylin and eosin (H&E)**.

## Key concepts
- **Tissue** – a community of similar cells found together in one part of the body, functioning together.
- **Extracellular matrix (ECM)** – network of proteins and other components secreted by cells that occupies the space between cells; abundance and composition are tissue-specific.
- **Anchoring junctions** – cell-to-environment junctions linked to the intracellular cytoskeleton, especially intermediate filaments; include desmosomes (cell-to-cell) and adhesions to the ECM.
- **Occluding junctions** – seal gaps between adjacent cells to form a barrier; example: tight junctions in some epithelia.
- **Channel-forming junctions** – create pores/channels connecting the cytoplasm of adjacent cells, allowing passage of ions and small molecules; example: gap junctions in cardiac muscle.
- **Signal-transmitting junctions** – specialised contacts allowing chemical communication, e.g. synapses between neurons.
- **Epithelial tissue** – continuous layer(s) of cells attached side-by-side, creating a barrier between two compartments.
- **Connective tissue** – tissue with a low density of cells and abundant ECM; functions include protection, connecting organs, energy storage and support.
- **Muscle tissue** – tissue of specialised contractile cells that generate movement and heat.
- **Nervous tissue** – tissue made of neurons and neuroglia that detects changes, integrates information and induces responses.
- **Basal lamina** – the specialised basal ECM found at the basal side of an epithelium.
- **Simple vs stratified epithelium** – one layer of cells vs more than one layer; pseudostratified has one layer with nuclei at different levels.
- **Exocrine gland** – secretory structure that releases products to an external surface/lumen.
- **Endocrine gland** – secretory cells of epithelial origin that lose contact with the surface and secrete hormones into the body interior.
- **Merocrine/apocrine/holocrine secretion** – secretion by exocytosis; secretion by loss of part of the cell; secretion by destruction of the whole cell.
- **Organ** – structure composed of two or more tissue types with a specific function, recognisable by shape and location.
- **Epithelial membrane** – an epithelium plus an underlying connective tissue working together.
- **H&E stain** – haematoxylin and eosin staining used to observe tissue/cell organisation under the microscope.

### From cells to tissues: extracellular matrix and cell adhesion
A tissue is a community of similar cells found together in one part of the body. Because different cells function together, they need to communicate and interact with each other and with the surrounding components. [00:01:19]

A fundamental part of this interaction is how cells are maintained together. Around cells there is a network of proteins and other components secreted by the cells in the area; this material occupies the space between cells and is called the **extracellular matrix (ECM)**. The abundance and composition of the ECM varies and is specific for each tissue type. [00:02:00]

For a multicellular organism, cells must become attached and stabilise in position. Two main attachment modes exist:

1. **Direct cell-to-cell attachment** via cell junctions.
2. **Indirect attachment**: each cell attaches to the ECM rather than directly to the next cell.

Most cells in the body do not move freely; exceptions include some immune/phagocytic cells, which move more freely. [00:03:19]

### Functional types of cell junctions
Cell junctions are grouped by function: [00:05:23]

1. **Anchoring junctions**
   - Connect cells to their environment, either ECM or another cell.
   - They are linked to cytoskeletal filaments, especially **intermediate filaments**, which have a structural role.
   - Membrane proteins link the extracellular environment to the intracellular cytoskeleton.
   - Examples:
     - **Desmosomes** and **adherens junctions**: cell-to-cell anchoring.
     - Other anchoring junctions bind cells to the ECM (the professor showed an example of ECM attachment).

2. **Occluding junctions**
   - Seal the gaps between adjacent cells.
   - This tight binding prevents movement of substances from one side to the other, creating a barrier.
   - Example: **tight junctions**, found in some epithelia. [00:07:07]

3. **Channel-forming junctions**
   - Create a porous structure/channel bridging the cytoplasm of two adjacent cells.
   - Allow the exchange of ions and small molecules between cells.
   - Example: **gap junctions**, also present in cardiac muscle. [00:08:27]

4. **Signal-transmitting junctions**
   - Specialised cell contacts for transmitting signals.
   - Example: **chemical synapses**, where two neurons establish contact and the attachment allows chemical communication. [00:09:30]

### The four basic tissue types
The body contains four basic tissue types. Two have highly specialised cells (**muscle** and **nervous**), while the other two are defined more by how cells are organised (**epithelial** and **connective**). [00:10:36]

1. **Epithelial tissue**
   - Formed by cells aggregated side-by-side into a surface/barrier.
   - Separates two compartments: one side contacts one part of the body, the other side contacts another part.
   - Found on the external skin surface, in hollow organs such as stomach/intestine, and in vessels. [00:11:37]
   - Function: regulates interaction/exchange of components between two spaces, e.g. external environment and interstitial fluid.

2. **Connective tissue**
   - Usually creates spaces between other tissues; found between tissues.
   - Functions: protection, keeping organs together, energy storage, and other roles.
   - Defined by **few cells and abundant ECM** between them, opposite to epithelial tissue. [00:12:55]

3. **Muscle tissue**
   - Composed of specialised cells able to contract, becoming shorter and thicker.
   - Contraction generates body movement, and because contraction requires energy, some energy is released as heat. [00:14:10]

4. **Nervous tissue**
   - Composed of **neurons** and **neuroglia (glia)**.
   - Detects changes in the body, integrates information, and induces responses, e.g. contacting muscles to contract. [00:15:12]

### Epithelial tissue in detail
Epithelial cells are disposed in a continuous layer, but their morphology can vary: [00:16:24]

- **Squamous**: flat cells.
- **Cuboidal**: cube-shaped cells.
- **Columnar**: cylindrical cells.

Adjacent epithelial cells attach by cell-to-cell junctions, which may include anchoring junctions, occluding junctions, and channel-forming junctions. Cells also attach to the ECM on the basal side. The epithelium has little ECM between its cells. [00:17:39]

The basal ECM forms the **basal lamina**, always found near the basal part of the epithelium. For example, in the intestine the apical side faces the lumen, while the basal side contacts the rest of the body. [00:18:46]

Epithelial cells are **polarised**: the apical surface and basal surface are very different. [00:19:54]

Epithelia are also classified by the number of layers:

- **Simple epithelium**: one layer of cells.
- **Stratified epithelium**: more than one layer.
- **Pseudostratified epithelium**: one layer of cells, but not all cells are aligned at the same position, so it looks stratified.

Combining shape and layering gives names such as **simple columnar epithelium** or **stratified squamous epithelium**. [00:21:00]

#### Functions of epithelia
1. **Protection / lining**: e.g. skin as a protective barrier with the external environment.
2. **Secretion**: specialised **glandular epithelium**.

Glandular epithelium can organise in different ways: [00:22:02]

- **Single secretory cells scattered within the epithelium**, e.g. **goblet cells** (caliciform cells) in the intestine, each secreting content into the intestinal lumen.
- **Secretory cells grouped into a specialised part of the epithelium**, forming a **gland**.

Exocrine glands arise as invaginations of an epithelium with secretory cells at the base/bottom, releasing secretions to an external surface or lumen (e.g. skin surface or intestinal lumen). [00:23:16]

During embryogenesis, some epithelial invaginations separate from the surface epithelium. These cells then secrete into the body interior rather than to the exterior, becoming **endocrine glands**. Most endocrine glands secrete **hormones**, which have important communication functions. [00:24:17]

#### Modes of exocrine secretion
Exocrine glands are classified by how secretions are produced: [00:25:15]

1. **Merocrine secretion**
   - Products are made in vesicles, vesicles fuse with the membrane, and contents are released by exocytosis.
   - Example: **sweat glands**.

2. **Apocrine secretion**
   - The cell loses part of itself as the secretion.
   - The cell grows and a portion separates; the cell remains alive.
   - Example: **mammary glands** producing milk.

3. **Holocrine secretion**
   - The whole cell is used as the secretion; the cell dies and all its components become the secretion.
   - Example: **sebaceous glands**, which produce the oily secretion on the skin surface.

Exocrine glands can also be classified by structure, e.g. tubular, branched/ramified tubule, coiled tubule, etc. [00:26:13]

### Connective tissue in detail
Connective tissue consists of a large amount of ECM with relatively few cells dispersed in it. The composition of the ECM determines the properties of each connective tissue. [00:28:18]

Types of connective tissue:

- **Embryonic connective tissue**: present only during development.
- **Proper connective tissue**: found between many organs; can be subdivided into **loose** and **dense** connective tissue. [00:29:20]
- **Supportive connective tissue**: provides structural support to the body.
  - **Cartilage**
  - **Bone**: cells in bone are dispersed, and the ECM contains minerals, but the overall cell/ECM organisation is still typical of connective tissue. [00:30:21]
- **Blood**: a liquid connective tissue.
  - Has the same defining pattern: few cells and abundant ECM.
  - But in blood the ECM is liquid, and cells move freely within it. [00:31:23]

### Muscle tissue in detail
Muscle cells are contractile. The contractile units are based on proteins **myosin and actin** (discussed in later sessions). Muscle functions include movement of the body, movement of contents within hollow organs, and heat generation. [00:31:40]

Three types of muscle: [00:32:24]

1. **Skeletal muscle**
   - Cells are large and have **more than one nucleus**, because each mature cell forms by fusion of several precursor cells (a syncytium).
   - Requires nerve activation to contract.
   - Under voluntary control.

2. **Cardiac muscle**
   - Cells are **mononucleated** and **branched/ramified** rather than cylindrical.
   - Cells attach to each other through specialised junction structures (e.g. intercalated discs/gap junctions seen later).
   - Contraction is involuntary; we cannot control heart rhythm.

3. **Smooth muscle**
   - Contractile cells found in **hollow organs** and **around vessels**.
   - Controls movement of internal fluids/substances through hollow organs and vessels.
   - Involuntary.

### Nervous tissue in detail
Nervous tissue contains two broad cell types: neurons and neuroglia. [00:34:44]

#### Neurons
- Highly differentiated cells with three main parts:
  - **Soma** (cell body)
  - **Dendritic arbor** (dendrites)
  - **Axonal compartment** (axon)
- Neurons are **excitable**: they generate electrical currents in the cell membrane, producing **action potentials** that propagate through the cell.
- Very diverse in size and shape; can be distinguished by dendritic branching pattern, etc.
- Neurons **do not divide by mitosis** (according to the professor). [00:34:50]

#### Neuroglia
- As abundant as neurons.
- Can divide throughout life, unlike neurons.
- Non-excitable cells.
- Smaller than neurons.
- Six types, distributed between the central and peripheral nervous systems:
  1. **Astrocytes**
  2. **Oligodendrocytes**
  3. **Microglia**
  4. **Ependymal cells**
  5. **Schwann cells**
  6. **Satellite cells**

Some are in the central nervous system, others in the peripheral nervous system. [00:35:51]

### Organs and histology
Different tissues can work together. An **epithelial membrane** always consists of an epithelial tissue attached to an underlying connective tissue working together. Because epithelial cells are tightly attached to each other, the epithelium itself cannot receive blood vessels; nutrients arrive from the surrounding connective tissue. [00:36:52]

An **organ** is a structure of the body composed of **two or more types of tissue** with a very specific function. Most organs are recognised by their shape and location. [00:38:24]

To analyse the inner structure of an organ:

1. Obtain a piece of tissue, e.g. by **biopsy** (liver, muscle, etc.).
2. **Fixate** it to prevent destruction and maintain proteins in place.
3. **Stain** with specific dyes to visualise structures such as the nucleus, membrane, or interstitial components.

A common method is **haematoxylin and eosin (H&E)** staining, which allows differentiation of cells in almost any tissue. Thin stained slices are observed under the microscope. Examples visible in preparations: artery, nerve, vein, muscle, and epithelium with subjacent connective tissue. [00:40:36]

## Self-test
1. Define a tissue. How does the extracellular matrix contribute to tissue organisation?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
A tissue is a community of similar cells found together in one part of the body, functioning together. The ECM is the network of proteins and other secreted components between cells; its composition is tissue-specific and cells attach to each other directly or to the ECM, which stabilises their positions. [00:01:19]
</details>

2. Compare epithelial and connective tissue in terms of cell density and ECM.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Epithelial tissue has many cells closely attached side-by-side, with very little ECM between them. Connective tissue has relatively few cells dispersed in abundant ECM. [00:12:55]
</details>

3. Match each junction to its function: anchoring, occluding, channel-forming, signal-transmitting.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Anchoring junctions attach cells to the ECM/other cells and link to the cytoskeleton (desmosomes, adherens junctions). Occluding junctions seal gaps between cells to form a barrier (tight junctions). Channel-forming junctions create channels between cytoplasms for ions/small molecules (gap junctions). Signal-transmitting junctions allow chemical communication (chemical synapses). [00:05:23]
</details>

4. Why do epithelial cells have polarity, and what is the basal lamina?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Epithelial cells form a barrier, so their apical surface faces one compartment (e.g. external environment or lumen) and the basal surface faces the body; these two surfaces are very different, making the cell polarised. The basal lamina is the specialised extracellular matrix found at the basal side of the epithelium. [00:18:46]
</details>

5. Classify the following epithelia: one flat layer; many flattened layers; one columnar layer with nuclei at different heights.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Simple squamous; stratified squamous; pseudostratified columnar. [00:21:00]
</details>

6. Explain the difference between exocrine and endocrine glands.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Exocrine glands release their secretion to an external surface or a lumen (e.g. skin surface, intestinal lumen), usually via an invagination of the epithelium. Endocrine glands separate from the surface during development and secrete into the body interior; most secrete hormones. [00:23:16]
</details>

7. Give one example for each exocrine secretion mode: merocrine, apocrine, holocrine.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Merocrine: sweat glands (exocytosis of vesicles). Apocrine: mammary glands (part of the cell is released). Holocrine: sebaceous glands (whole cell dies and becomes the secretion). [00:26:13]
</details>

8. Why are bone and blood classified as connective tissues?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Because they share the defining connective tissue organisation: few cells dispersed in abundant extracellular matrix. In bone the ECM is mineralised; in blood the ECM is liquid and cells move freely. [00:30:21]
</details>

9. Compare skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle on: nuclei, voluntary control, and location/function.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Skeletal: multinucleated (syncytium), voluntary, requires nerve activation, attached to skeleton/movement. Cardiac: mononucleated, branched, involuntary, heart; cells attached by specialised junctions. Smooth: found in hollow organs and around vessels, involuntary, controls movement of contents/fluids. [00:32:24]
</details>

10. What are the main differences between neurons and neuroglia?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Neurons are excitable, highly differentiated, have soma/dendrites/axon, generate action potentials, and do not divide by mitosis. Neuroglia are non-excitable, smaller, as abundant as neurons, can divide throughout life, and include astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia, ependymal cells, Schwann cells and satellite cells. [00:34:44]
</details>

11. Define an organ. Why must an epithelium be associated with connective tissue?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
An organ is a body structure composed of two or more tissue types with a specific function. Epithelium is too tightly packed to receive blood vessels, so nutrients arrive from the underlying connective tissue; the combination is called an epithelial membrane. [00:37:57]
</details>

12. What are the standard steps to prepare a tissue sample for microscopic observation, and what does H&E stand for?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Obtain a piece of tissue (biopsy), fixate it to preserve structure, stain with dyes, cut thin slices, and observe under the microscope. H&E is haematoxylin and eosin, a common stain that differentiates cells in almost every tissue. [00:39:50]
</details>
