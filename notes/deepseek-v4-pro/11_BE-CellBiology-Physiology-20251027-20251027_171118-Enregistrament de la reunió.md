## Summary

This session continued the cellular functions topic by going through cell division in detail: the ordered cell cycle, mitosis for somatic cells, and meiosis for gamete formation. It then covered how cells die, contrasting necrosis with programmed cell death (apoptosis), including the caspase cascade and the extrinsic and intrinsic pathways that trigger it. Finally, the professor introduced cell differentiation, explaining how cells with identical genomes become functionally specialised. Key takeaways:

- The cell cycle is regulated at specific checkpoints to ensure faithful DNA replication and chromosome segregation [00:02:51].
- Mitosis gives two diploid daughter cells with identical genomic material; it proceeds through prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase, and cytokinesis [00:05:10].
- Meiosis is the reductive division that produces haploid gametes; meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes, and meiosis II separates sister chromatids [00:13:17].
- Apoptosis is a controlled, silent form of cell death that avoids inflammation, unlike necrosis [00:27:08].
- Apoptosis is executed by a proteolytic caspase cascade that can be triggered by external signals (extrinsic pathway) or internal damage such as mitochondrial cytochrome c release (intrinsic pathway) [00:34:59].
- Different cell types arise from the same genome by differential gene expression during differentiation [00:45:17].

## Key concepts

- **Interphase** – the part of the cell cycle in which the cell grows and replicates its DNA; divided into G1, S, and G2 phases [00:00:00].
- **M phase** – the phase in which duplicated DNA and cell components are segregated into two daughter cells [00:01:46].
- **Checkpoint** – a regulatory point in the cell cycle where the cell can pause or check that processes have been completed correctly [00:02:51].
- **Chromatid** – one copy of a replicated chromosome; two sister chromatids are joined at the centromere before segregation [00:07:39].
- **Centromere** – the region where two sister chromatids are attached [00:07:39].
- **Centrosome** – the microtubule-organising structure at each pole of the mitotic spindle [00:09:36].
- **Metaphase plate** – the equatorial plane where chromosomes align during metaphase [00:09:36].
- **Cytokinesis** – division of the cytoplasm and organelles to fully separate the two daughter cells [00:06:37].
- **Diploid** – having two copies of each chromosome; human somatic cells have 23 pairs, i.e. 46 chromosomes [00:14:24].
- **Haploid** – having one copy of each chromosome; human gametes have 23 chromosomes [00:15:37].
- **Tetrad** – the paired alignment of homologous chromosomes during metaphase I of meiosis [00:20:15].
- **Homologous recombination** – exchange of corresponding segments between homologous chromosomes during meiosis I [00:22:46].
- **Necrosis** – cell death by membrane rupture and uncontrolled release of cell contents, causing inflammation [00:27:08].
- **Apoptosis** – programmed cell death in which the cell shrinks and its contents are degraded internally without inflammation [00:28:17].
- **Caspases** – proteases that cleave specific proteins and execute the apoptotic cascade [00:34:59].
- **Procaspases** – inactive caspase precursors that become activated by cleavage [00:37:46].
- **Death receptors** – cell-surface receptors that trigger the extrinsic apoptosis pathway when bound by extracellular signals [00:39:14].
- **Differentiation** – the process by which cells with the same genome become specialised by using different sets of genes [00:45:17].

## Detailed notes

### Cell cycle and checkpoints [00:00:00–00:04:06]

The cell cycle is the ordered mechanism by which cells reproduce. It has two broad parts:

- **Interphase**: cell growth and DNA synthesis. It is subdivided into:
  - **G1 phase**
  - **S phase** – new DNA synthesis/replication
  - **G2 phase**
- **M phase**: segregation of the duplicated DNA and cell components into two daughter cells.

Some cells can exit the cycle and become non-dividing cells. The typical example given by the professor is **neurons**, which may not divide again [00:01:46].

The cell cycle is regulated at specific checkpoints because the cell must ensure that the new cells contain the appropriate genomic material. The professor highlighted three positions:

1. A checkpoint before DNA synthesis (start point) [00:02:51].
2. A checkpoint before chromosome segregation in M phase [00:02:51].
3. Another checkpoint within M phase to regulate the finishing of division [00:02:51].

### Mitosis [00:04:07–00:13:16]

Somatic cells, which are most of the cells of the body, divide by mitosis. One mother cell produces two daughter cells with the exact same genetic material as the original cell. DNA duplication occurs during interphase; mitosis then segregates the duplicated DNA equally. The professor focused on genomic content, noting that daughter cells may not always be identical in all cellular composition, but they are identical in genomic content [00:05:10].

The phases of mitosis are:

- **Prophase** [00:06:37–00:08:46]:
  - Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes.
  - Each replicated chromosome consists of **two chromatids** joined at the **centromere**.
  - The nuclear envelope disassembles, releasing chromosomes into the cytoplasm.

- **Metaphase** [00:08:47–00:09:35]:
  - Condensed chromosomes align at the equatorial **metaphase plate**.
  - Each chromatid is oriented toward one of the two poles of the cell.
  - At each pole there is a **centrosome**, from which microtubules arise and attach to chromosomes.

- **Anaphase** [00:09:36–00:10:37]:
  - The centromere linking the two chromatids splits.
  - Each chromatid moves toward one centrosome/pole, pulled by microtubules.
  - After separation, each chromosome consists of only one chromatid.

- **Telophase** [00:10:37–00:11:56]:
  - Chromosomes reach the poles and decondense.
  - New nuclear envelopes assemble around each set of chromosomes.
  - The cell begins to divide.

- **Cytokinesis** [00:11:56–00:13:16]:
  - The cytoplasm is divided, completing separation of the two daughter cells.
  - A **contractile ring** of cytoskeletal structures forms and pinches the cell in the middle.
  - Completion of cytokinesis gives two fully independent daughter cells, each able to enter a new interphase.

### Meiosis [00:13:17–00:27:08]

Meiosis occurs only for sexual reproduction, producing gametes in the reproductive system (spermatozoa in the testicles and oocytes in the ovaries) [00:13:17].

Human somatic cells are **diploid**: they have 23 pairs of chromosomes, i.e. two copies of chromosome 1, two copies of chromosome 2, and so on. Gametes, however, must be **haploid**, carrying only one copy of each chromosome. Fertilisation combines two haploid gametes to restore a diploid cell that can then divide by mitosis [00:14:24–00:16:44].

Meiosis begins like mitosis: the cell goes through interphase, grows, and replicates its DNA. Therefore, at the start of meiosis each chromosome also contains two chromatids. The difference appears after DNA duplication in how chromosomes are distributed [00:17:56].

#### Meiosis I [00:19:02–00:23:49]

- During **prophase I**, the nuclear envelope disappears and chromosomes condense, each chromosome containing two chromatids.
- During **metaphase I**, chromosomes do not align individually. Instead, homologous chromosomes pair side by side as **tetrads** at the metaphase plate.
- During **anaphase I**, whole homologous chromosomes separate to opposite poles, not individual chromatids. Each chromosome still contains two chromatids because the centromere is not lost.
- After **telophase I and cytokinesis I**, two haploid cells are formed. Each cell has 23 chromosomes, but each chromosome still consists of two chromatids linked at the centromere.

An important event during meiosis I is **homologous recombination** [00:22:46]. Homologous chromosomes aligned at the metaphase plate can exchange segments. This generates new combinations of genes in the next generation. The professor stressed that:

- Recombination does **not** create new gene forms or mutations; it only creates new combinations of existing genes.
- Exchange occurs only between **homologous** chromosomes and homologous regions (e.g. chromosome 1 with the other chromosome 1), not between non-equivalent chromosomes.

#### Meiosis II [00:23:49–00:25:59]

Meiosis II is much more similar to mitosis.

- In each of the two haploid cells from meiosis I, chromosomes align individually at the metaphase plate.
- During anaphase, the two **sister chromatids** of each chromosome separate to opposite poles.
- After telophase and cytokinesis, up to **four haploid gametes** are produced from one original cell.

The professor noted that in the testicles this process produces up to four spermatozoa. In oocytes, the process is more complex and only one of the four possible cells typically reaches the final functional stage [00:25:59].

#### Comparison of mitosis and meiosis [00:25:59–00:27:08]

- Mitosis: one diploid cell with duplicated DNA → **two diploid daughter cells**.
- Meiosis: one diploid cell with duplicated DNA → up to **four haploid gametes**.
- Mitosis resembles **meiosis II** more closely; **meiosis I** is the specific reductive division that generates haploid cells.

### Cell death: necrosis vs apoptosis [00:27:08–00:30:57]

Cells can die by two very different processes:

1. **Necrosis** [00:27:08–00:28:17]:
   - The cell is damaged or broken, e.g. by physical injury to the cell membrane.
   - Intracellular contents spill into the surroundings.
   - This promotes **inflammatory responses** in the surrounding tissue.
   - It is not the typical, controlled way cells die.

2. **Apoptosis** [00:28:17–00:30:57]:
   - The cell shrinks and condenses.
   - Contents are degraded from the inside, not spilled.
   - Also called **programmed cell death**.
   - During apoptosis: cytoskeleton collapses, nuclear envelope disassembles, chromatin/DNA is broken into fragments, and specific intracellular compartments degrade components.
   - Surrounding cells can engulf and degrade the residual parts.
   - There is **no inflammatory response**, so it is a very organised and silent way of dying.

Apoptosis is necessary for correct physiology. Examples given:

- During development, it eliminates the tissue between fingers and toes to shape the digits [00:30:58].
- It adjusts cell numbers: cells are often overproduced and some must be eliminated, e.g. nerve cells that arrive at their target but are in excess [00:32:14].
- It can eliminate abnormal, misplaced, or non-functional cells [00:32:14–00:33:47].

### Caspase cascade and apoptosis pathways [00:33:47–00:44:02]

The initial death signal can arise from different points, but apoptosis always involves a **cascade of reactions**. Each activated step activates the next, and the signal is amplified at each step [00:33:47].

The key proteins are **caspases**, which are **proteases**: proteins that cleave other specific proteins. Before activation, caspases exist as inactive **procaspases**. Cleavage activates them, and the active caspase then cleaves and activates the next caspase in the cascade [00:34:59–00:37:46].

The professor stressed that once the caspase cascade starts, it is difficult to stop because each active caspase amplifies the signal further. The final result is degradation of intracellular components: cytosolic proteins, nuclear components, and other essential parts of the cell [00:37:46].

#### Extrinsic pathway [00:37:46–00:41:53]

- Initiated by **external signals** detected by cell-surface receptors.
- These receptors are called **death receptors** because their activation signals the cell to die.
- When an extracellular signal binds a death receptor, the signal is transferred into the cell and activates the caspase cascade.

Cells can receive both death-inducing and **protective** signals. For example, neurons that reach the correct target may receive protective signals through membrane receptors; neurons that fail to arrive or arrive late and do not receive protection may undergo apoptosis. Thus, the outcome can depend on the balance between death and survival/protection signals [00:40:44].

#### Intrinsic pathway [00:41:53–00:44:02]

- Initiated by **internal signals**, often because the cell recognises damage that could be dangerous, e.g. loss of mitochondrial integrity.
- If the outer mitochondrial membrane is broken, contents of the intermembrane space are released into the cytosol.
- A key example is **cytochrome c**. Normally it functions in mitochondria for energy production, but if it escapes to the cytosol, it triggers apoptosis.
- Cytochrome c promotes aggregation of the protein **APAF1**, which leads to activation of the caspase cascade.
- It is called intrinsic because the signal originates inside the very same cell that will die.

### Cell differentiation [00:44:03–00:46:35]

Not all cells in the body are equivalent. Multicellular organisms are organised so that different body parts have different functions, and this requires cells with different properties. The process by which a cell becomes specialised is called **differentiation** [00:45:17].

Even though all cells have the same genomic material, different cell types use different sets of genes. The set of genes being transcribed in each cell type, at each moment, is responsible for the cell’s function. The professor noted:

- Some cells are already committed to a differentiated fate after being born.
- Other cells change their gene expression and function only transiently.
- Approximately **200 different cell types** are present in the human body [00:46:35].

## What the professor stressed

- Cell cycle regulation is **important** to ensure that new cells contain the appropriate genomic material; there are checkpoints before DNA synthesis, before chromosome segregation, and within M phase [00:02:51].
- During mitosis, proper **alignment at the metaphase plate** and separation of chromatids are key to understanding how genetic material is segregated equally [00:09:36].
- Homologous recombination in meiosis is **important for generating new combinations of genes**, but it does **not create new mutations** and occurs only between homologous chromosomes [00:22:46].
- Apoptosis is important because it allows cell death **without spilling contents** and therefore **without inflammation**; surrounding cells engulf the remains [00:29:48].
- The caspase cascade is an **amplifying**, step-by-step process that is **difficult to stop once started** [00:37:46].
- Cells can integrate **death and survival signals**; protection signals can prevent apoptosis, for example in developing neurons [00:40:44].

3. During metaphase of mitosis, how are chromosomes arranged, and why does this matter for anaphase?  
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Chromosomes align at the metaphase plate, with each chromatid oriented toward opposite poles. 4. What is the main difference between metaphase of mitosis and metaphase I of meiosis?  
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
In mitosis, individual replicated chromosomes align at the metaphase plate. In metaphase I of meiosis, homologous chromosomes pair side by side as tetrads. Then, in anaphase I, whole homologous chromosomes separate, not sister chromatids. [00:20:15–00:21:25]
</details>

5. What is homologous recombination, and what does it achieve?  
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It is the exchange of corresponding segments between homologous chromosomes during meiosis I. It generates new combinations of existing genes in the next generation. It does not create new gene forms or mutations, and it occurs only between homologous chromosomes and homologous regions. [00:22:46]
</details>

6. Compare necrosis and apoptosis in terms of cell contents and inflammation.  
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Necrosis involves membrane rupture and spilling of intracellular contents into the surroundings, promoting inflammation. Apoptosis involves cell shrinkage, internal degradation of contents, and no spilling; surrounding cells can engulf the remains, so there is no inflammatory response. [00:27:08–00:29:48]
</details>

7. Why is the caspase cascade considered an amplifying process that is hard to stop?  
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Caspases are proteases that cleave and activate other caspases. Each active caspase can activate several downstream procaspases, so the signal is amplified step by step. Once the cascade starts, this positive amplification makes it difficult to stop. [00:34:59–00:37:46]
</details>

8. Distinguish the extrinsic and intrinsic pathways of apoptosis.  
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The extrinsic pathway begins with external signals binding to death receptors on the cell surface, which then activate the intracellular caspase cascade. The intrinsic pathway begins with internal damage, such as mitochondrial outer membrane damage releasing cytochrome c into the cytosol; cytochrome c promotes APAF1 aggregation and activation of the caspase cascade. [00:37:46–00:44:02]
</details>

9. Why do some developing neurons die even though they have reached the correct target area?  
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Developing neurons can receive both death-inducing and protective signals. Cells that reach the target in time receive protection signals and survive. Cells that arrive late or do not receive the protective signal may receive only the death signal and undergo apoptosis. It is a balance between induction and protection. [00:40:44]
</details>

10. How can the same genome give rise to approximately 200 different cell types?  
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
All cells have the same genomic material, but different cell types use different sets of genes. The set of genes being transcribed in a given cell type at a given moment determines its function. This process of becoming specialised is called differentiation. [00:45:17–00:46:35]
</details>
