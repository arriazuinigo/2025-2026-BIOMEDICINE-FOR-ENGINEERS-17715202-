## Summary

This final session of the course completed the **endocrine system** review and covered the **nervous system** in more detail, then introduced **homeostasis and physiopathology**. The endocrine part focused on how hormones act as chemical messengers, the main endocrine glands, and the recurring three-level regulatory axis: hypothalamus → pituitary (hypophysis) → third organ, illustrated with the female reproductive cycle. The nervous system part covered CNS/PNS organisation, neuron and neuroglia structure, action potentials, synaptic transmission, integration/summation in neural circuits, and sensory/motor pathways. The session ended with definitions of homeostasis, internal vs external environment, negative and positive feedback, and basic clinical concepts such as disorder, disease, symptoms, signs and diagnosis.

Key takeaways:

- Hormones are chemical signals released into interstitial fluid or blood that act only on cells with the appropriate receptor.
- Many endocrine axes use a three-level control system: hypothalamus, hypophysis/adenohypophysis, and a peripheral gland, usually with negative feedback.
- Neurons are excitable, post-mitotic, polarised cells: many dendrites receive input, one axon transmits output.
- Action potentials depend on voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels; myelin speeds propagation.
- Integration means a neuron sums many excitatory and inhibitory synaptic inputs and fires only if threshold is reached.
- Homeostasis is maintained by feedback systems; negative feedback is the usual mechanism, while positive feedback is rare and amplifies a response, as in childbirth.

- **Releasing/inhibiting hormones** — hypothalamic neuroendocrine signals that stimulate or inhibit adenohypophyseal hormone secretion [00:07:41].

- **Neuroglia** — support cells of the nervous system; they divide and provide support, nutrition, protection and myelination [00:33:35].
- **Myelin sheath** — insulating wrapping around axons produced by oligodendrocytes (CNS) or Schwann cells (PNS) that accelerates action potential propagation [00:37:02].
- **Neurotransmitter** — chemical released from a presynaptic terminal that binds receptors on a specific postsynaptic cell, the most common form of synaptic transmission [00:39:??].
- **Integration/summation** — the process by which a neuron combines many synaptic inputs; only if the summed depolarisation reaches threshold is an action potential generated [00:42:56].

## Detailed notes

### 1. Endocrine system: general principles

The endocrine system consists of specialised organs whose main function is hormone production, although almost all body organs produce some hormones [00:01:02]. Its main role, together with the nervous system, is to coordinate the different body systems so the body functions as a whole [00:02:32].

Hormones are released into the interstitial fluid between cells and transmit information to other cells. Some target cells are nearby; other hormones enter the bloodstream and reach distant parts of the body [00:03:12]. Regardless of whether a hormone is hydrosoluble or liposoluble, it always needs a receptor, typically a protein on/in the target cell, for the cell to react [00:04:19].

### 2. Hypophysis and hypothalamic control

The **hypophysis (pituitary)** is a small organ below the brain that links the CNS and the endocrine system [00:05:36].

- **Neurohypophysis (posterior pituitary)**: produces **oxytocin** and **antidiuretic hormone (ADH)**. These hormones are produced by neurons whose axons end in this area; they release hormone into interstitial space rather than making a synapse [00:05:36].
- **Adenohypophysis (anterior pituitary)**: produces **growth hormone (GH)**, **thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)**, **adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)**, **prolactin**, **follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)** and **luteinizing hormone (LH)** [00:06:39].

Adenohypophyseal hormone release is controlled by the **hypothalamus**. Hypothalamic neuroendocrine cells produce releasing hormones that stimulate adenohypophyseal secretion, or inhibiting hormones that suppress it [00:07:41].

### 3. Three-level axes: hypothalamus → hypophysis → third organ

Most pituitary hormones act on a third organ, creating a three-step regulatory system [00:08:30]:

- **Adrenal gland** (above the kidney): releases **cortisol** only when ACTH arrives from the adenohypophysis. Other adrenal hormones such as **aldosterone, epinephrine and norepinephrine** are released without that adenohypophyseal connection [00:09:49].
- **Thyroid gland**: releases **T3 and T4** after TSH arrives; T3/T4 regulate metabolism. **Calcitonin** is also produced by the thyroid without adenohypophyseal control [00:09:49].
- **Parathyroid glands** (posterior of the thyroid): produce **parathyroid hormone (PTH)**. Together, calcitonin and PTH regulate circulating calcium levels [00:10:57].

This axis arrangement allows inter-regulation, usually through negative feedback [00:14:38].

### 4. Pancreas and gonads

The **pancreas** has an exocrine function — secreting pancreatic juice for digestion in the small intestine — but also contains **islets of Langerhans**, which form the endocrine pancreas. These islets produce **insulin** and **glucagon**, which regulate blood glucose [00:11:59].

**Ovaries and testicles** are not pure endocrine glands because they also produce germ cells, but their hormone production is important. Ovaries produce mainly **estrogen and progesterone**; testicles produce **testosterone** [00:13:36].

Sex hormone regulation again follows the three-level axis [00:14:38]:

1. Hypothalamus releases **gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH)**.
2. GnRH stimulates the adenohypophysis to secrete **FSH** and **LH**.
3. FSH/LH act on ovaries/testes to stimulate the final sex hormones: testosterone in males, estrogens and progesterone in females.

Negative feedback from the final hormones regulates the whole system [00:14:38].

### 5. Example: female reproductive cycle

The reproductive cycle lasts about **25–28 days** and illustrates the GnRH–FSH/LH–ovary axis [00:16:09]:

- Early in the cycle, the hypothalamus releases **GnRH**, which activates **FSH** secretion.
- FSH reaches the ovaries and stimulates growth of the cells associated with the oocyte; these cells produce **estrogen**.
- Rising estrogen acts on the **uterus**, making the **endometrium proliferate** and increase in thickness [00:16:09].
- Later, **LH** also increases; when LH reaches the ovary, it triggers **ovulation**, releasing the oocyte [00:17:59].
- After ovulation, the cells that surrounded the oocyte continue producing **estrogen** and also begin producing **progesterone**. Progesterone maintains the endometrium in a **secretory phase** for about 14 days, producing components a fertilized oocyte might need [00:18:33].
- If fertilization does not occur, these ovarian cells degenerate, hormone production falls, the endometrium dies and is shed as **menstruation**, and a new cycle begins [00:19:49].

### 6. Nervous system: organisation and functions

The nervous system has two main components [00:20:51]:

- **Central nervous system (CNS)**: the encephalon (brain, cerebellum, brainstem) and spinal cord.
- **Peripheral nervous system (PNS)**: nerves, ganglia, enteric plexuses and sensory receptors outside the protection of the cranium and vertebrae.

Functions of the nervous system are coordination — detecting sensory information, processing it in the CNS, and activating motor responses [00:20:51].

The **sensory division** obtains information from the environment or body and transfers it to the CNS. It includes **somatic senses** (temperature, pain, touch) and **special senses** (vision, hearing, taste) [00:22:05].

The **motor division** sends outputs from the CNS to effectors. It has:

- **Somatic nervous system**: voluntary; activates skeletal muscle.
- **Autonomic nervous system**: involuntary; controls smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands and the enteric system around the digestive tube [00:23:10].

### 7. CNS structure: gray and white matter

The brain and cerebellum are folded structures in which neurons are arranged in layers. For example, in the cerebellum, Purkinje cells form a well-structured layer above granular cells; this layered arrangement is important for brain complexity [00:24:??].

In a brain slice, particularly the cerebral cortex, there are two visible regions:

- **Gray matter**: contains neuron cell bodies/somas (where the nucleus is). In the brain, gray matter is on the outside.
- **White matter**: contains axons that connect neurons. In the brain, white matter is on the inside [00:26:24].

In the **spinal cord**, the distribution is the opposite: **gray matter is interior**, containing neuron cell bodies, and **white matter is external**, containing axons that project to and from the encephalon [00:27:33].

### 8. Peripheral nervous system components

The PNS contains [00:29:13]:

- **Nerves**: bundles of axons, some originating in the CNS, travelling to targets such as muscles.
- **Ganglia**: protuberances containing neuron cell bodies with nuclei; these are specific sensory neurons and autonomic motor neurons.
- **Sensory receptors**: specialised cells that detect sensory changes.
- **Enteric plexuses**: networks of nerves.

### 9. Cells of the nervous system: neurons and neuroglia

**Neurons** are abundant in brain and spinal cord. They are excitable cells that can generate **action potentials**. They are **post-mitotic** (typically do not divide) and have a polarised structure: a **soma** containing the nucleus, plus **dendrites** and **axons** [00:30:51].

Neuron structure varies. Purkinje cells of the cerebellum have a highly ramified dendritic arbour, while pyramidal cells of the cortex look different. A typical neuron has **many dendrites** but **one single axon**; the axon may branch, but there is only one axon origin from the cell body [00:31:57].

**Neuroglia** are found in similar numbers to neurons and provide support, nutrition and protection. They do divide. In the CNS they include:

- **Astrocytes**: support and nutrition.
- **Oligodendrocytes**: produce myelin sheaths.
- **Microglia**: immune-related protection (macrophages of the CNS).
- **Ependymal cells**: participate in the blood–brain barrier, forming a border between the CNS and other body tissues [00:33:35].

In the PNS the corresponding glial cells are **Schwann cells**, which produce myelin, and **satellite cells**, which support neurons [00:34:40].

### 10. Action potential and myelination

Neurons generate electrical stimuli called action potentials. At rest, the neuron has a negative membrane potential. If the membrane potential is altered enough to reach **threshold**, **voltage-gated sodium channels** open. Na⁺ enters the cell, causing **depolarisation**. Then **voltage-gated potassium channels** open, K⁺ leaves the cell, and the membrane potential is **restored** [00:34:40].

Action potentials occur at one point and typically propagate along the whole neuron. Because axons can connect to very distant places, this electrical signal enables fast communication over long distances [00:37:02].

**Myelin sheaths**, produced by oligodendrocytes (CNS) and Schwann cells (PNS), isolate the axon from the environment. This insulation makes the electrical stimulus propagate faster [00:37:02].

### 11. Synaptic transmission

Information flow in a neuron is directional: dendrites receive information, the soma integrates, and the axon transmits the electrical stimulus rapidly to the axon terminals [00:38:25].

When the action potential arrives at the distal axon, the neuron usually releases a chemical compound into the **synapse**. This chemical communication is directed to one specific attached cell. Chemical synapses are more common than electrical (gap junction) synapses. The released chemical is a **neurotransmitter**, and it requires specific receptors on the second cell [00:39:??].

Most synapses occur between the axon of one neuron and a dendrite of another; a postsynaptic neuron may receive many synaptic inputs on its dendrites but has one axon that branches to contact many downstream neurons [00:41:15].

### 12. Integration, summation and circuits

**Integration** is the process that decides whether a neuron fires given the many synaptic inputs it receives. A neuron may receive thousands of inputs at different times and locations. Each small input alone may be insufficient, but inputs arriving together can summate and reach threshold [00:42:56].

Resting potential can be pushed closer to threshold by **excitatory inputs** or pushed away by **inhibitory inputs**. Only when combined excitatory signals arrive at the same moment and region and the summed depolarisation reaches **threshold** is an action potential generated. Once generated, it propagates along the axon and transmits information to the next neuron [00:44:56].

Most neural processing involves **circuits**, i.e. functional pools of interconnected neurons linked by synapses. Integration occurs at each neuron in the circuit, so sensory input does not always produce a motor response — it depends on the summed activity of the network [00:48:22].

### 13. Sensation, perception and motor output

**Sensory receptors** are specialised structures that detect environmental changes (chemical composition, pressure, light, etc.). Detection by sensory receptors can produce **sensations**, the conscious detection of changes in the external or internal environment. Sensations include general somatic sensations (cold, heat, pain, visceral internal sensations) and special senses (smell, taste, vision, hearing, balance) [00:49:27].

**Perception** is more complex: it is the conscious interpretation of sensations [00:51:19].

Motor responses are produced by the motor division. Skeletal muscle activation occurs only at the **neuromuscular junction**, a synapse between a motor neuron and a skeletal muscle cell. The neurotransmitter is **acetylcholine**; when released, the muscle cell detects it and responds with contraction [00:52:22].

### 14. Homeostasis: internal vs external environment

**Homeostasis** is the process whereby the internal environment of an organism tends to remain balanced and stable — for example, regulated temperature, glucose and calcium levels [00:52:22].

The **internal environment** is the fluid inside the body but outside cells. About **two-thirds** of total body fluid is intracellular; the remaining extracellular fluid is the internal environment. It is mainly **interstitial fluid**, along with **lymph**, **cerebrospinal fluid** and **plasma** [00:55:28].

The **external environment** is any place outside the body, but also internal compartments that connect directly to the outside because they are not interstitial fluid or plasma. Examples given: the mouth, stomach, intestine lumen, air in the lungs, and liquids in the urinary system [00:56:??]. The external environment is not controlled by the body, while the internal environment is tightly regulated. Large external temperature changes cause only minimal internal changes thanks to homeostatic mechanisms [00:58:20].

Homeostasis is mainly coordinated by the **nervous and endocrine systems** [00:59:27].

### 15. Feedback systems

Homeostatic control always has the same general structure [01:00:29]:

- A **regulated variable** (e.g. blood pressure, calcium concentration, temperature).
- A **receptor/sensor** that detects changes in the variable.
- An **afferent pathway** sending information to a **control centre**.
- The control centre, sometimes nervous and sometimes endocrine, integrates and sends **efferent signals** to effectors.

This is called a **feedback system**; it continuously supervises, controls, evaluates and modifies the regulated variable [01:00:29].

**Negative feedback** is the most common type. The response reverses the change produced by the stimulus [01:02:50]. Examples:

- **Blood pressure**: increased pressure is detected by receptors; the control centre reduces heart rate, lowering blood pressure.
- **Glucose**: high glucose triggers insulin secretion to lower glucose; low glucose triggers glucagon secretion to raise glucose [01:03:57].

**Positive feedback** is rare and amplifies the stimulus. The example used was **childbirth**: stretching of the cervix activates oxytocin production from the neurohypophysis; oxytocin causes uterine contractions; contractions push the baby against the cervix, increasing stretching; this produces more oxytocin and stronger contractions. The loop continues until an external input interrupts it, specifically birth [01:06:06].

### 16. From homeostasis to disease

An **imbalance** is instability in maintaining homeostasis; if it occurs, it may lead to disorder or disease [01:08:28].

- **Disorder**: any abnormality in structure or function of the body.
- **Disease**: a specific disorder characterised by a set of distinguishable symptoms and signs.
- **Systemic disease**: a disease that affects many parts of the body simultaneously [01:09:30].
- **Symptom**: a subjective change in body function, not apparent or detectable by a physician; something only the patient can describe.
- **Sign**: an objective change in body function/structure that a physician can detect.
- **Diagnosis**: recognising a disorder or disease by studying the patient’s symptoms and signs together [01:10:56].

## Self-test

1. Why is the lumen of the stomach considered part of the external environment, while interstitial fluid is part of the internal environment?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The internal environment is the extracellular fluid inside the body but outside cells: mainly interstitial fluid, lymph, cerebrospinal fluid and plasma. The stomach lumen is directly connected to the exterior and is not interstitial fluid or plasma, so it is classified as external environment even though it is inside the body. The same applies to lung air and urinary tract liquid.
</details>

2. A patient has damage to oligodendrocytes. Which part of the nervous system is affected, and what consequence would you expect for action potential propagation?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Oligodendrocytes are CNS glial cells that produce myelin sheaths around CNS axons. Loss of oligodendrocytes causes demyelination in the brain/spinal cord; action potentials propagate more slowly or may fail, impairing fast communication in the CNS.
</details>

3. In the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, high cortisol levels would be expected to feed back and suppress which upstream hormones?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
High cortisol should feed back negatively to reduce hypothalamic releasing hormone (CRH-type signal, as general releasing hormone) and adenohypophyseal ACTH secretion, thereby reducing further cortisol production. The professor emphasised that these three-level axes are regulated by negative feedback.
</details>

4. Why is the posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis) unusual as an endocrine structure?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Its hormones — oxytocin and antidiuretic hormone — are produced by neurons whose axons end in the posterior pituitary. The neurons release hormone into interstitial space rather than making a typical synapse.
</details>

5. A neuron receives one excitatory input on a distal dendrite and two inhibitory inputs near the soma. Based on summation, will it fire? Explain.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Probably not. Excitatory inputs bring the membrane potential closer to threshold; inhibitory inputs move it away. Because inhibition is near the soma and there are two inhibitory inputs, the net summation would likely prevent threshold from being reached. The neuron fires only if the summed depolarisation at the trigger zone reaches threshold.
</details>

6. Compare the locations of gray and white matter in the cerebral cortex and the spinal cord.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
In the cerebral cortex, gray matter is outside (cell bodies) and white matter is inside (axons). In the spinal cord the pattern is reversed: gray matter is central and white matter is peripheral.
</details>

7. Explain why positive feedback during childbirth is useful but also dangerous if not interrupted.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Positive feedback amplifies the stimulus: cervix stretching → oxytocin release → uterine contraction → more stretching. This produces progressively stronger contractions needed for birth. It is dangerous because it continues to amplify unless an external event (birth itself) interrupts the loop.
</details>

8. How does the pancreas carry out both exocrine and endocrine functions?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Most of the pancreas secretes pancreatic juice into the small intestine (exocrine digestion). Within the pancreas, the islets of Langerhans form the endocrine pancreas, producing insulin and glucagon to regulate blood glucose.
</details>

9. A patient tells the doctor “I feel very tired,” but the doctor observes nothing measurable. Is “tiredness” a symptom or a sign? Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It is a symptom, because it is a subjective change felt by the patient that the physician cannot directly detect or measure. A sign would be an objective change the physician can observe or measure.
</details>

10. If blood glucose falls too low, which endocrine response restores homeostasis, and is this negative or positive feedback?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Low glucose triggers glucagon secretion from the endocrine pancreas, which activates metabolism to release glucose into the bloodstream. This is negative feedback because it opposes the original change and returns glucose toward normal.
</details>

11. A drug blocks voltage-gated sodium channels in neurons. What will happen to action potentials and synaptic transmission?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Blocking voltage-gated Na⁺ channels prevents the depolarisation phase of the action potential. Without action potentials, the signal cannot propagate along the axon, so neurotransmitter release at the synapse will not be triggered and transmission to the postsynaptic cell fails.
</details>

12. Why can myelin increase the speed of action potential propagation?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Myelin sheaths insulate the axon from the surrounding environment. This insulation allows the electrical stimulus to propagate faster along the axon than in unmyelinated fibres.
</details>
