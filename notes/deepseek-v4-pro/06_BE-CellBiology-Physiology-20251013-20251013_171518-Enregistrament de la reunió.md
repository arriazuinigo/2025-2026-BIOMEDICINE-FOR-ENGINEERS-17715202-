## Summary

This session continued the cell biology block by moving from the plasma membrane into the **cytoplasm** and **nucleus**, then began the U4-2 organelle slides. The professor explained that the cytosol is a dense protein-packed fluid where metabolism and signalling occur, described the three cytoskeletal filament systems and their molecular motors, then covered nuclear structure, chromatin, and the flow of genetic information via replication, transcription and translation. The second half introduced protein synthesis by ribosomes (with rRNA and tRNA), chaperone-assisted folding, proteasome-mediated protein degradation, and vesicular trafficking through exocytosis and endocytosis.

- Cytosol is not simple water; it is a dense mesh of enzymes, cytoskeleton and other proteins where most metabolic and signalling reactions take place.
- The three cytoskeletal systems differ in composition, diameter, location and transport ability: microfilaments and microtubules transport, intermediate filaments are only structural.
- The nucleus has a double membrane with pores and stores DNA packaged as chromatin; replication and transcription occur inside, translation occurs in the cytosol.
- Ribosomes are RNA-based machines; rRNA and tRNA are essential for decoding mRNA into protein, and chaperones help new proteins fold correctly.
- The proteasome degrades tagged, non-functional or unneeded proteins; failure of degradation is linked to neurodegenerative diseases.
- Vesicular trafficking uses membrane compartments (ER, Golgi, vesicles, plasma membrane) to move proteins and other components into and out of the cell via exocytosis and endocytosis.

## Key concepts

- **Cytoplasm** [00:00:57]: Everything between the plasma membrane and the nucleus; contains the cytosol and organelles.
- **Cytosol** [00:00:57]: The liquid portion between organelles; a dense environment full of proteins, enzymes, cytoskeleton and other molecules; site of many chemical reactions.
- **Organelles** [00:00:57]: Structures, most of them membrane-delimited, that perform particular functions inside the cell.
- **Metabolism** [00:03:39]: The whole set of chemical reactions taking place inside the cell; divided into catabolic and anabolic pathways.
- **Catabolic pathways** [00:05:19]: Reactions that break large/high-molecular-weight molecules into smaller pieces, producing energy and small building blocks.
- **Anabolic pathways** [00:06:30]: Reactions that build larger molecules from building blocks; they require energy.
- **Signalling pathways** [00:06:30]: Intracellular reaction cascades activated when an extracellular molecule binds to a transmembrane protein and the information—not the molecule itself—is transferred across the membrane.
- **Cytoskeleton** [00:09:04]: Dynamic filamentous structures formed by protein subunits; provides structural support and, in some types, tracks for transport.
- **Molecular motors** [00:14:40]: Proteins that move specific cargoes along cytoskeletal tracks; myosin, kinesin and dynein.
- **Nuclear envelope** [00:19:16]: The double membrane (inner and outer) that borders the nucleus, with a perinuclear space between the two membranes.
- **Nuclear pore** [00:20:33]: Specialized structure that regulates movement of substances between cytosol and nucleus.
- **Chromatin** [00:21:05]: Complex of DNA with proteins, mainly histones, that packages DNA inside the nucleus.
- **Euchromatin / heterochromatin** [00:22:39]: Less-compacted / more-compacted forms of chromatin.
- **Replication** [00:22:39]: Duplication of genomic DNA inside the nucleus before cell division.
- **Transcription** [00:24:52]: Synthesis of RNA using DNA as a template, inside the nucleus.
- **Translation** [00:24:52]: Synthesis of proteins using the information in messenger RNA, in the cytosol.
- **Ribosome** [00:30:32]: Large catalytic complex, made mainly of ribosomal RNA, that synthesises proteins.
- **Chaperones** [00:34:34]: Proteins that stabilise and protect unfinished polypeptides, then allow correct final 3D folding.
- **Proteasome** [00:37:02]: Complex/organelle that recognises and degrades tagged proteins into pieces, generating reusable amino acids.
- **Exocytosis** [00:42:57]: Process by which vesicle contents are released to the plasma membrane/exterior.
- **Endocytosis** [00:42:57]: Process by which substances are internalised from the extracellular space into vesicles.

## Detailed notes

### Cytoplasm and cytosol [00:00:57]

Cytoplasm is defined as everything between the plasma membrane and the nucleus. It has two main parts:

- **Cytosol**: the liquid portion.
- **Organelles**: internal structures, most of them also membrane-delimited, that carry out particular functions.

The cytosol is the liquid found between organelles, and it is where many chemical reactions occur during the life of the cell. The professor compared the cell to a factory in which many chemical reactions take place simultaneously [00:02:31].

Much of this chemistry requires enzymes, so the cytosol contains many dissolved proteins. It also contains the **cytoskeleton**, proteins that maintain cell structure and transport organelles and substances across the cell [00:02:31].

The professor stressed that the cytosol is not “water and that’s it”: it is a dense mesh of proteins, as shown in the molecular-representation slide [00:03:39]. These proteins include enzymes that regulate and perform metabolic reactions, plus structural and transport proteins.

### Metabolism: catabolic and anabolic pathways [00:03:39]

The entire set of chemical reactions inside the cell is called **metabolism**. It is divided into two main types:

- **Catabolic reactions/pathways** [00:05:19]:
  - Convert high-molecular-weight molecules into smaller pieces.
  - Tend to produce smaller molecules and **energy**.
  - Some energy is released as heat; some is used by the cell for other purposes.
  - Produce building blocks, e.g. a protein processed into amino acids that can be reused to build new proteins.

- **Anabolic reactions/pathways** [00:06:30]:
  - Build larger molecules from smaller building blocks.
  - Require energy.

The professor showed a metabolic map: each point is a compound that can be converted into another using an enzyme. Many metabolic pathways exist inside the cell, and most—but not all—occur in the cytosol [00:06:30].

### Signalling pathways in the cytosol [00:06:30]

Another kind of chemical reaction occurs in the cytosol: **signalling pathways**. Plasma membrane proteins that cross the membrane can bind a molecule on the outside. What is transferred across the membrane is **not the molecule itself, but the information** that binding has occurred [00:06:30–00:07:53].

That binding activates a series of intracellular chemical reactions: the signalling pathway. Signalling pathways are related to, but distinct from, metabolic pathways, and they also happen in the cytosol.

### Cytoskeleton: the three filament types [00:09:04]

The cytoskeleton is made of **dynamic filamentous proteins**: protein subunits attach one after another to form long multimeric filaments. There are three types:

1. **Microfilaments** [00:09:04–00:10:57]:  
   - Made of subunits of the protein **actin**.
   - Thin: **9 nm** in thickness.
   - Typically found just below the plasma membrane.
   - Help maintain cell shape, especially in protrusions such as **intestinal microvilli**.
   - Also move organelles and substances over short distances near the membrane.

2. **Intermediate filaments** [00:10:57]:  
   - Slightly thicker: **10 nm**.
   - Composition varies: different proteins depending on the cell type.
   - Function is **purely structural**; never related to transport.
   - Found throughout the whole cytosol, maintaining the entire internal distribution of the cell.

3. **Microtubules** [00:12:09]:  
   - Made of the protein **tubulin** in **alpha and beta** subunits.
   - Form a tubular structure made of **13 protofilaments**, with a hollow inner part.
   - Have both structural and transport roles, particularly transport of organelles.
   - In many cells, microtubules are organised from a single point, the **centrosome** (the professor corrected himself when initially calling it a “nucleus”; it is the microtubule-organising centre, not the cell nucleus).

### Molecular motors and intracellular transport [00:13:31]

Only microfilaments and microtubules have transport roles; intermediate filaments do not. The filaments act as **roads/tracks** for moving organelles. To actually move a cargo (a vesicle, a mitochondrion, etc.), the cell needs additional proteins called **molecular motors** [00:14:40].

- **Myosin motors**: move organelles along **microfilaments**.
- **Kinesin motors**: move organelles along **microtubules** in one direction—from the **minus end to the plus end** (microtubules have polarity).
- **Dynein motors**: also use **microtubules**, but move in the **other direction**.

The system is complex because motors can be specific for cargo (e.g. a vesicle vs. a mitochondrion) and for the cytoskeletal type/direction. All this movement requires **energy**, produced by the cell [00:17:11].

### The nucleus: structure and exceptions [00:17:11]

Human cells typically have **one nucleus per cell**, but there are exceptions:

- **Erythrocytes** (red blood cells) have **no nucleus**.
- **Polynucleated cells** (cells with more than one nucleus) exist; they typically form by **fusion of previously uninucleated cells** [00:18:15].

In the typical case, all the cell’s DNA/genetic material is contained in the nucleus. The nucleus is limited by the **nuclear envelope** [00:19:16]:

- It is a **double membrane** (inner and outer), similar in nature to the plasma membrane.
- Between inner and outer membranes there is a **space** that the cell uses.
- **Nuclear pores** are specialised structures that regulate movement of substances from cytosol to nucleus and vice versa [00:20:33].
- Inside the nucleus there may be a specialised region, the **nucleolus**, whose specific function is the **synthesis of ribosomes** [00:20:33–00:21:05].

### Chromatin and DNA packaging [00:21:05]

Inside the nucleus, genetic information is stored as **DNA**:

- DNA is **double-stranded and linear in eukaryotes**.
- It is maintained as more than one molecule: chromosomes.
- Human cells have **46 chromosomes**.
- DNA interacts with specialised proteins, especially **histones**, to form **chromatin**; this stabilises and packages DNA.
- Differences in compaction create:
  - **Euchromatin**: less compacted regions.
  - **Heterochromatin**: more compacted regions.

### Replication, transcription and translation [00:22:39]

These are the central processes for genetic information:

- **Replication** [00:22:39]: When a mother cell divides into two daughter cells, the genomic material must be duplicated. Replication produces a second copy of the DNA, and occurs **inside the nucleus**.
- **Transcription** [00:24:52]: The cell uses the DNA sequence as a pattern to synthesise **RNA**. This occurs **inside the nucleus**. Transcription creates different RNA types, the most abundant being **messenger RNA (mRNA)**.
- **Translation** [00:24:52]: mRNAs leave the nucleus through the pores, arrive at the cytosol, and in specialised areas the information in the RNA is used to synthesise proteins.

The professor stressed that **proteins are the most important functional products of the cell**: they are needed for replication, transcription and translation themselves [00:24:52].

### Protein synthesis: ribosomes, RNAs and chaperones [00:28:23]

Protein synthesis consists of joining monomeric **amino acids** into a long polymeric chain. This complex process occurs in specialised places called **ribosomes** [00:29:28–00:30:32].

The **ribosome** is a large catalytic complex made mainly from **RNA**—the main example of RNA acting as a functional product itself [00:30:32]:

- **Messenger RNA (mRNA)** contains the information for protein synthesis.
- **Ribosomal RNA (rRNA)** forms the large and small ribosomal subunits and helps mRNA create proteins. The catalytic/functional core is RNA, not protein.
- **Transfer RNA (tRNA)** helps decode information from nucleotides into amino acids: every **three nucleotides** are linked to **one specific amino acid** [00:31:56–00:33:06].

Amplification [00:33:06]: from one gene, many mRNA molecules can be synthesised; from each mRNA molecule, many protein molecules can be synthesised. The professor showed an electron microscopy image of a single mRNA with multiple ribosomes along it, each ribosome producing a growing protein chain; at the end of the mRNA, the protein is almost complete.

**Chaperones** [00:34:34] are specialised proteins that help during synthesis:

- They stabilise/protect the unfinished protein until the ribosome finishes.
- Once synthesis is complete, chaperones allow the protein to fold into its correct final **3D structure**.
- This is especially important for proteins whose 3D structure requires bringing together the initial portion and the last portion of the chain; without chaperones, such complex proteins could not fold properly.

### Protein degradation: the proteasome [00:36:01]

Not all newly formed proteins fold correctly, and even correctly folded proteins may later lose function or become unnecessary. They need to be degraded [00:37:02].

If non-functional proteins are not degraded and accumulate, they can create **pathology**. The professor gave the example of **many neurodegenerative diseases** [00:37:02].

The organelle responsible for protein degradation is the **proteasome**:

- It is formed by proteins that recognise proteins to be eliminated.
- Proteins destined for degradation are **tagged with specific labels** via chemical reactions during the life of the protein.
- The label is recognised by the proteasome; the protein is attached and degraded, cut into pieces.
- This generates new **amino acids** that can be reused or used for energy [00:37:02–00:39:25].

The process is well regulated: it must avoid degrading useful proteins while ensuring degradation of all unuseful proteins.

### Vesicular trafficking: exocytosis and endocytosis [00:39:25]

Many cell components must move from one side of the cell to the other. For example, proteins made in ribosomes need to reach the plasma membrane. To control movement and membrane composition, the cell uses membrane-bound compartments [00:39:25–00:41:49]:

- **Endoplasmic reticulum**
- **Golgi apparatus**
- **Vesicles**
- **Plasma membrane**

These compartments ensure delivery of components to the plasma membrane and uptake of components from the plasma membrane for use or degradation [00:41:49].

Two opposing processes were introduced:

- **Exocytosis** [00:42:57]: components contained in vesicles arrive at and fuse with the plasma membrane, releasing their contents to the outside. This can be used for signalling or for producing components delivered to the extracellular space.
- **Endocytosis** [00:42:57]: components from the extracellular space are taken up into the cell as vesicles.

Both processes change the membrane composition of the cell and exchange substances between the inside and outside.

The professor previewed that the next session would discuss the role of exocytosis/endocytosis in moving substances across the cell, more membrane organelles, non-membrane organelles, and mitochondria [00:44:11].

## What the professor stressed

- The cytosol is **not just water**: it is a dense mesh of proteins, enzymes, cytoskeleton and other components [00:03:39].
- The three cytoskeletal filaments have distinct characteristics; **intermediate filaments are purely structural and never transport**, whereas microfilaments and microtubules also transport [00:10:57–00:13:31].
- Movement of organelles on cytoskeletal tracks **requires energy** [00:17:11].
- **Proteins are the most important functional products** of the cell; they are needed for replication, transcription and translation themselves [00:24:52].
- Protein synthesis includes several RNAs: **mRNA, rRNA and tRNA**, with rRNA acting as a catalytic/functional RNA [00:30:32].
- Gene-to-protein is an **amplification process**: one gene → many mRNA molecules → many protein molecules per mRNA [00:33:06].
- **Chaperones are essential** for correct 3D folding of complex proteins [00:34:34].
- Failure to degrade non-functional proteins can produce pathology, for example in **neurodegenerative diseases**; proteasome degradation is tightly regulated [00:37:02].
- Exocytosis and endocytosis both modify **plasma membrane composition** and exchange substances between inside and outside [00:42:57].

## Self-test

1. Why should the cytosol be thought of as a dense mesh of proteins rather than simply water?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Because it contains many dissolved enzymes that carry out metabolic reactions, signalling proteins, and the cytoskeleton that maintains cell structure and transports organelles. The professor stressed that the cytosol is full of proteins, not just a watery compartment.
</details>

2. Distinguish catabolic and anabolic pathways in terms of molecular size and energy.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Catabolic pathways break large/high-molecular-weight molecules into smaller pieces and produce energy (some as heat, some usable building blocks such as amino acids). Anabolic pathways build larger molecules from smaller building blocks and require energy.
</details>

3. Compare microfilaments, intermediate filaments and microtubules: subunit, diameter, location and transport role.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Microfilaments: actin subunits; 9 nm; just below the plasma membrane and in protrusions such as intestinal microvilli; structural and short-distance transport near the membrane. Intermediate filaments: composition varies by cell type; 10 nm; throughout the cytosol; purely structural, never transport. Microtubules: alpha/beta tubulin; 13 protofilaments forming a hollow tube; structural and long-distance organelle transport, usually organised from the centrosome.
</details>

4. How do molecular motors use the cytoskeleton, and what distinguishes myosin, kinesin and dynein?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Cytoskeletal filaments act as tracks/roads. Molecular motors bind specific cargoes (e.g. a mitochondrion or vesicle) and move them along those tracks. Myosin uses microfilaments. Kinesin uses microtubules and moves from the minus end to the plus end. Dynein also uses microtubules but moves in the opposite direction. The movement requires energy.
</details>

5. What are the exceptions to the “one nucleus per cell” rule mentioned in class?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Erythrocytes (red blood cells) have no nucleus. Polynucleated cells have more than one nucleus and are typically formed by fusion of previously uninucleated cells.
</details>

6. Why is the nuclear envelope described as a double membrane, and what is the function of nuclear pores?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The nuclear envelope has an inner and an outer membrane with a space between them. Nuclear pores are specialised structures that regulate movement of substances between the cytosol and the nucleus.
</details>

7. Trace the flow of genetic information from DNA to protein, naming each process and where it occurs.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
DNA is replicated inside the nucleus before cell division. During transcription, RNA is synthesised in the nucleus using DNA as a template; the most abundant product is mRNA. mRNA exits through nuclear pores into the cytosol. During translation, ribosomes in the cytosol use the mRNA information to synthesise proteins.
</details>

8. What is the role of chaperones during protein synthesis, and why are they needed for complex proteins?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Chaperones stabilise and protect unfinished proteins until the ribosome completes synthesis, then allow the protein to fold into its correct 3D structure. They are needed because some proteins form complex structures that require the beginning and end of the chain to come together; without chaperones, such proteins could not fold correctly.
</details>

9. How does the proteasome decide which proteins to degrade, and why is correct protein degradation important?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Proteins destined for degradation are tagged with specific labels through chemical reactions during their lifetime. The proteasome recognises the label, attaches the protein, and cuts it into pieces, generating amino acids that can be reused or used for energy. Correct degradation is essential because accumulation of non-functional proteins can cause pathology, e.g. in many neurodegenerative diseases.
</details>

10. Compare exocytosis and endocytosis and explain their effect on the plasma membrane.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Exocytosis delivers vesicle contents to the plasma membrane and releases them outside; endocytosis takes extracellular components into the cell as vesicles. Both change the membrane composition of the cell and exchange substances between the inside and outside, using membrane compartments such as the ER, Golgi, vesicles and plasma membrane.
</details>

11. Explain why protein production from a single gene is considered an amplification process.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
One gene can be transcribed into many mRNA molecules, and each mRNA molecule can be translated by multiple ribosomes at the same time. Thus, from one gene, many copies of the protein can be produced.
</details>

12. How does signalling across the plasma membrane differ from transporting the signalling molecule itself into the cell?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
In signaling pathways, an extracellular molecule binds to a transmembrane protein. What crosses the membrane is the information that binding has occurred, not the molecule itself. This binding then activates a series of intracellular chemical reactions in the cytosol.
</details>
