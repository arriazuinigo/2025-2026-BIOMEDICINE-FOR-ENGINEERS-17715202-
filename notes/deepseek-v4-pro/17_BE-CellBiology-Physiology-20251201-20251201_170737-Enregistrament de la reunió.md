- **QRS complex** — electrical change representing ventricular contraction (ventricular systole). [00:01:03]

- **Isovolumetric contraction** — phase in which ventricles contract with all valves closed, so pressure rises but blood does not yet move. [00:02:33]
- **Isovolumetric relaxation** — phase after ejection when ventricles relax with all valves closed, before ventricular filling begins. [00:04:40]
- **Arteries / veins / capillaries** — vessels carrying blood from the heart to tissues / from tissues to the heart / the thin exchange vessels in between. [00:05:45]
- **Lymph** — interstitial fluid collected into lymphatic vessels; it has no cells and few proteins. [00:12:49]
- **Primary vs secondary lymphoid organs** — primary organs supply lymphocytes (red bone marrow, thymus); secondary organs monitor the internal environment and initiate immune responses (lymph nodes, spleen, lymphatic follicles). [00:14:15–00:17:44]

- **Adaptive immunity** — selective, has memory; includes B-cell humoral response (antibodies) and T-cell cellular response (killing infected cells). [00:21:01–00:26:04]
- **Pneumocytes type I / type II** — type I form the thin alveolar exchange surface; type II produce pulmonary surfactant and can divide/differentiate into type I. [00:31:17–00:32:27]

- **Respiratory membrane** — the very thin combined wall of type I pneumocyte and capillary endothelium where gas exchange occurs. [00:33:52]

## Detailed notes

### Electrical and mechanical events of the cardiac cycle

The heart’s electrical activity is recorded on the body surface as three main waves: the **P wave**, the **QRS complex**, and the **T wave**. [00:00:00]

The **P wave** represents the start of contraction of the atria. When the atria contract, the blood they contain moves into the ventricles. This occurs in both the right and left sides of the heart. This phase can be called atrial systole. [00:00:00–00:01:03]

The **QRS complex** initiates ventricular contraction. When the ventricles contract, pressure inside them increases. The valve between the atrium and the ventricle closes, and pressure builds up in the ventricle while blood does not yet move. This is **isovolumetric contraction**. [00:01:03–00:02:33]

Once pressure in the ventricle is high enough, the valve that allows blood to leave the heart opens. Blood is ejected from the heart in the **ventricular ejection** phase. This occurs in parallel on the right and left sides: the right ventricle ejects blood toward the pulmonary arteries, and the left ventricle ejects blood through the aorta. [00:02:33–00:03:35]

The **T wave** represents relaxation of the cardiac muscle cells. All cells relax and return to the initial state. First there is **isovolumetric relaxation**: the ventricles stop ejecting blood, and the valve between the artery and the ventricle closes to prevent backflow, so the ventricle relaxes with all valves closed. [00:03:35–00:04:40]

After the ventricle has relaxed, pressure inside it becomes low enough that the valve between the atrium and the ventricle opens and blood flows in: **ventricular filling**. The first part of filling occurs while the ventricle is still relaxed; the next cycle begins with atrial contraction completing filling. [00:04:40–00:05:45]

So cardiac mechanical activity has five phases associated with three electrical events: contraction of atria, contraction of ventricles, and relaxation. [00:05:45]

### Blood vessels: structure and pressure

Blood leaving the heart travels through **arteries**, which carry blood from the heart to the tissues. **Veins** carry blood from the tissues back to the heart. Both have the same general three-layer structure: [00:05:45–00:06:51]

- **Tunica intima** — the endothelium, which forms the barrier between blood and tissues, together with its basal lamina.
- **Tunica media** — smooth muscle and some connective tissue; the smooth muscle can contract.
- **External tunica** — connective tissue.

Arteries and veins differ: veins have much less smooth muscle because the pressure in veins is much lower than in arteries. Veins also contain valves at intervals; these valves allow blood to flow in only one direction, back toward the heart, and close if blood tries to flow backward. [00:06:51–00:07:54]

**Capillaries** are the small-caliber vessels between arteries and veins where exchange between blood and tissue occurs. Their caliber is only slightly larger than an erythrocyte, which permits efficient movement of substances across the vessel wall. [00:07:54–00:09:17]

Pressure in arteries is much higher than in veins, with capillaries in between. Large arteries show a pulsatile pressure: about 110–120 mmHg during ventricular ejection and 70–80 mmHg during relaxation. Pressure decreases as arteries ramify toward capillaries and then declines further in veins. [00:09:17–00:10:22]

### Lymphatic and immune system: organization

The lymphatic/immune system has dispersed components: lymphatic vessels that drain lymph, lymphoid tissues such as the spleen, thymus, tonsils, and lymph nodes, and immune cells — especially leukocytes in the blood. Its main function is protection against pathogens. [00:10:22–00:11:22]

Lymphatic vessels begin in the tissues as thin vessels similar to capillaries. They collect interstitial fluid from between the cells. This fluid, once inside the lymphatic vessels, is called **lymph**; it has no cells and few proteins. The vessels contain valves so lymph moves in only one direction. Lymph eventually returns to the bloodstream at the **thoracic duct** in the thorax. On its way, lymph passes through tissues such as lymph nodes, where the immune system surveys its contents and can react. [00:11:22–00:14:15]

Lymphoid organs are divided into primary and secondary. **Primary lymphoid organs** supply other organs with lymphocytes: the **red bone marrow**, where all blood cells including lymphocytes are generated (hematopoiesis), and the **thymus**, where some lymphocytes undergo a maturation step. **Secondary lymphoid organs** monitor the internal environment and initiate immune responses: **lymph nodes** along lymph drainage, the **spleen** inserted into the blood vascular system, and **lymphatic follicles** near the mucous membranes of the digestive, respiratory, and urinary tracts — places most vulnerable to pathogen entry. [00:14:15–00:17:44]

### Innate immunity

The immune system can respond in two ways. The **innate immune system** is fast, short-lasting, non-selective, and lacks memory. It responds the same way whether the body has been exposed once or many times to the same pathogen. [00:17:44–00:19:49]

The **first line of defense** is physicochemical barriers: the skin and mucosa physically block passage, and they may secrete chemical components that make pathogen entry more difficult. [00:19:49]

If a pathogen enters the body, the **second line of defense** includes cells and proteins inside the body. Innate immune cells include phagocytes, natural killer cells, neutrophils, basophils, and eosinophils. Neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils contain granules — vesicles with components inside. When they react to a pathogen, they release these granule contents onto the pathogen, which can help stop the pathogen or attract other immune components. [00:19:49–00:21:01]

Proteins in blood also participate. The **complement system** is a complex machinery of blood proteins; its activation can create pores in the membrane of a bacterium, killing the cell. [00:21:01]

### Adaptive immunity and vaccination

The **adaptive immune system** is selective: it is oriented specifically against the particular pathogen that has entered. It also has **memory**. The first exposure produces a slow response, but later exposures to the same pathogen produce a much faster, stronger, and more effective response. It belongs to the second line of defense: it acts only after a pathogen has entered the internal medium. [00:21:01–00:22:22]

Adaptive immunity has two main ways of responding: [00:22:22–00:26:04]

- **B lymphocytes / humoral response** — B cells recognize specific proteins of the pathogen and produce **antibodies**. Antibodies attach selectively to those pathogen proteins, sometimes blocking them or helping phagocytes remove the pathogen.
- **T lymphocytes / cellular response** — T cells recognize and kill the body’s own cells that have been infected. Example: in COVID-19, tracheal mucosa cells infected with the virus are recognized and killed by T cells. This cytotoxicity targets only infected cells.

Both arms occur simultaneously and both have memory. This is why vaccination is important: vaccines train the system to produce memory, so when the real virus or pathogen infects the body, the second response is already trained, specific, and faster. [00:26:04–00:26:52]

### Respiratory system: anatomy and functions

The respiratory system includes the lungs and the upper respiratory tract: nasal cavity and pharynx, followed by the lower respiratory tract before the lungs: larynx and trachea. Inside the lungs, the trachea branches into two primary bronchi, which continue ramifying to carry air throughout the lungs. [00:28:53–00:30:03]

Trachea and bronchi contain cartilage in their walls, providing rigidity to the air conduits. The smallest bronchioles have no cartilage. The final ramifications are the **alveoli**, where gas exchange between blood and air occurs. [00:30:03–00:31:17]

Functions of the respiratory system include: [00:30:03–00:31:17]

- exchange of gases
- production of sounds
- regulation of blood pH (blood pH is mainly controlled by the lungs and kidneys)
- elimination of water and heat

### Alveoli and the respiratory membrane

Alveoli are bubble-like structures at the end of the air passages. Air arrives inside the bubble, and the wall is formed by a very thin epithelium. The main alveolar cells are **type I pneumocytes**, which create the main surface of the alveolus. [00:31:17–00:32:27]

**Type II pneumocytes** are also present. They can divide and differentiate to produce more type I pneumocytes; they are the stem cells for the type I population. Their important function is to produce **pulmonary surfactant**, a product containing lipids and phospholipids that prevents the lung from collapsing during repeated entry and exit of air. [00:32:27–00:33:52]

Immediately adjacent to the type I pneumocytes is the **endothelium of the capillaries**. The thin type I pneumocyte and the thin capillary endothelial cell, very close together, form the **respiratory membrane** where gas exchange occurs. [00:33:52]

Because oxygen and carbon dioxide are small, lipophilic, uncharged molecules, they cross the cell membranes by **simple diffusion** down their concentration gradients. The enormous number of alveoli and ramifications creates a very large exchange surface: about **70 square meters** per human. [00:33:52–00:35:23]

### Gas transport in blood

**Oxygen** is transported in two ways: [00:35:23–00:36:39]

- dissolved in the liquid of the blood — only about **1.5%** of the oxygen needed, because oxygen has low solubility;
- bound to **hemoglobin** inside erythrocytes — the main transport mechanism.

For oxygen to enter erythrocytes, it must first dissolve in plasma and then move from this dissolved pool into the red blood cells. [00:36:39]

**Carbon dioxide** is transported differently: [00:36:39–00:38:17]

- dissolved — a higher percentage than oxygen, because CO₂ is more soluble;
- as **bicarbonate** — dissolved CO₂ can be converted to carbonic acid and then to bicarbonate anion; this is the main/easiest way to transport CO₂;
- bound to proteins — about **23%** of CO₂ is bound to proteins. This binding is not to a specific site on a specific protein; any protein has chemical groups that can bind CO₂.

### External and internal respiration

Gas exchange in the lungs is called **external respiration**: oxygen enters the blood and carbon dioxide leaves the blood into the air, then is exhaled into the atmosphere. [00:38:17]

In tissues, a similar exchange occurs in the opposite direction. Blood arriving at tissues is loaded with oxygen bound to hemoglobin; this oxygen leaves the blood and reaches the cells, which need it. Metabolically active cells produce carbon dioxide as a residue of mitochondrial metabolism (electron transport chain, citric acid cycle). This CO₂ enters the bloodstream, is transported by the three mechanisms above, returns to the lungs, and is removed. This tissue-level exchange is **internal respiration** and also occurs by simple diffusion. [00:39:38–00:40:00]

### Ventilation mechanics and lung volumes

Inspiration requires muscle contraction. The main muscle is the **diaphragm**, supported by the **external intercostal muscles**. When these muscles contract, the diaphragm flattens/moves downward and the thoracic cavity enlarges, allowing the lungs to fill with air. [00:40:00–00:42:17]

In resting expiration, no contraction is needed: relaxation of the diaphragm and the other inspiratory muscles is enough. Forced expiration uses different muscles: the **abdominal muscles** and the **internal intercostal muscles**. [00:42:17]

In normal quiet breathing, the volume of air moved per breath is only about **500 mL** (tidal volume). Forced inspiration can bring in much more, and forced expiration can expel much more. These volumes are measured by spirometry. Even after maximal expiration, a **residual volume** remains in the lungs: the air present in the conducting passages — trachea, bronchi, bronchioles — which do not collapse during exhalation. This residual volume can never be expelled. [00:43:38–00:45:14]

### Ventilation–perfusion coupling

Efficient gas exchange requires a balance between airflow and blood flow. The normal ventilation–perfusion ratio (**V/Q**) is around **0.8–1**, allowing good diffusion and exchange. [00:45:14–00:46:21]

If ventilation is too low — for example because mucus, obstruction, or blockage prevents air from reaching the alveoli — the V/Q ratio falls below 0.8 or toward 0, producing a **shunt** situation. Gas exchange is reduced, and oxygen does not properly enter the blood. [00:46:21–00:47:25]

The opposite problem is when air reaches an area of lung normally but blood flow does not. This is **physiological dead space**, with V/Q above 1. [00:47:25]

The lungs have regulatory mechanisms to keep exchange efficient. In a hypoxic area of the lung, blood flow is reduced compared with well-ventilated areas, so perfusion is directed toward regions where oxygen arrives properly. [00:48:26]

## Self-test

1. Why does the cardiac cycle have five mechanical phases if the ECG shows only three main waves?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The ECG shows three electrical events — P (atrial depolarization), QRS (ventricular depolarization), and T (ventricular repolarization) — but the mechanical cycle includes pressure and valve changes that separate ventricular systole into isovolumetric contraction and ejection, and relaxation into isovolumetric relaxation and filling. Hence: atrial systole, isovolumetric contraction, ventricular ejection, isovolumetric relaxation, ventricular filling. [00:00:00–00:05:45]</details>

2. Explain why arteries have more smooth muscle than veins, and why veins have valves.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure, so they need more smooth muscle in the tunica media. Veins carry blood at much lower pressure, so they need less muscle. Veins have one-way valves to prevent backflow and ensure blood returns to the heart. [00:05:45–00:07:54]</details>

3. A molecule must cross the respiratory membrane from alveolar air to capillary blood. What two cell layers does it cross and why is no active transport needed for O₂ and CO₂?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It crosses the type I pneumocyte and the capillary endothelial cell. O₂ and CO₂ are small, lipophilic, uncharged molecules, so they cross by simple diffusion down their concentration gradients; no active transport is needed. [00:33:52]</details>

4. What would happen to alveolar stability if type II pneumocytes were lost or non-functional?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Without type II pneumocytes, pulmonary surfactant would not be produced. Surfactant prevents alveolar collapse during breathing, so its absence would make the lungs prone to collapse and impair gas exchange. Type II cells also generate new type I pneumocytes. [00:32:27]</details>

5. Compare the three ways carbon dioxide is transported in blood with the main way oxygen is transported.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
CO₂ is transported dissolved in plasma, as bicarbonate anion (the main way), and about 23% bound to proteins. O₂ is poorly soluble, so only about 1.5% is dissolved; the great majority is transported bound to hemoglobin inside erythrocytes. [00:35:23–00:38:17]</details>

6. Distinguish shunt from physiological dead space in ventilation–perfusion coupling.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Shunt occurs when ventilation is too low relative to perfusion (V/Q below 0.8), so blood passes through poorly ventilated lung and is not properly oxygenated. Physiological dead space occurs when ventilation is present but blood flow is absent (V/Q above 1), so the ventilated air does not participate in gas exchange. [00:46:21–00:47:25]</details>

7. Why is residual volume always present in the lungs, even after maximal forced expiration?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Residual volume is the air kept in the conducting airways — trachea, bronchi, bronchioles — that do not collapse during exhalation. It is the air in the transport channels themselves and cannot be expelled. [00:43:38–00:45:14]</details>

8. How does the innate immune system differ from the adaptive immune system in terms of speed, specificity, and memory?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Innate immunity is fast, short-lasting, non-selective, and has no memory: it responds the same way every time. Adaptive immunity is selective for the specific pathogen and has memory: the first response is slow, but later responses are faster and stronger. [00:17:44–00:21:01]</details>

9. Why does vaccination provide protection even though the vaccine is not the real disease?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Vaccination trains the adaptive immune system to generate memory against a pathogen without causing severe disease. When the real pathogen later infects the body, the memory response is already prepared, specific, faster, and stronger. [00:26:04]</details>

10. Trace the route of lymph from the interstitial space back to the blood, including where immune surveillance occurs.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Interstitial fluid enters thin lymphatic capillaries and becomes lymph. Lymph moves through lymphatic vessels with one-way valves, passes through lymph nodes where the immune system surveys its contents, and finally returns to the bloodstream at the thoracic duct in the thorax. [00:11:22–00:14:15]</details>

11. What are the two main processes performed by the nephron, according to the professor’s self-study assignment?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Filtration, which occurs in the initial part of the nephron, and reabsorption, which occurs in the rest of the nephron. [00:51:13–00:52:24]</details>
