## Summary
This session completed the locomotor system by explaining how skeletal muscle cells are organised and how contraction happens at the molecular level (sarcomeres, action potentials, calcium and filament sliding), and by classifying joints. It then started the cardiovascular system: blood as a liquid connective tissue, its cells and plasma, erythrocytes and haemoglobin, and the three mechanisms of haemostasis. The second half focused on the heart: its anatomy, the pulmonary and systemic circuits, the key distinction between arteries/veins and oxygenated/deoxygenated blood, cardiac muscle electrical coupling, autorhythmic cells, the ECG, and the five-step cardiac cycle.

Key takeaways:
- Muscle contraction results from calcium release triggering thin filaments to slide over thick filaments, shortening each sarcomere.
- Joints are classified as synarthrosis (immobile), amphiarthrosis (small movement), or synovial (free movement).
- Blood is a liquid connective tissue with plasma, erythrocytes, leukocytes and platelets, and performs transport, regulation and protection functions.
- Haemostasis has three distinct but interconnected mechanisms: vascular spasm, platelet plug, and coagulation (fibrin clot).
- Artery/vein refers only to direction of flow relative to the heart, not to oxygen content; pulmonary and systemic circuits differ.
- The heart beats autonomously because autorhythmic cells initiate action potentials that propagate through gap junctions between cardiac muscle cells.
- The cardiac cycle can be understood as five steps: atrial contraction, isovolumetric ventricular contraction, ventricular ejection, isovolumetric relaxation, and passive ventricular filling.

## Key concepts
- **Sarcomere**: the functional contractile unit of skeletal and cardiac muscle, made of thick and thin filaments arranged as thin–thick–thin; shortens during contraction.
- **Transverse (T) tubules**: invaginations of the muscle cell membrane that carry action potentials into the interior of the cell.
- **Sarcoplasmic reticulum**: muscle endoplasmic reticulum; stores and releases calcium when triggered by the action potential.
- **Synarthrosis**: immobile joint formed by connective tissue/ligaments, e.g. between cranial bones.
- **Amphiarthrosis**: joint allowing small movement, typically with a cartilaginous disc, e.g. between vertebrae.
- **Synovial joint**: freely movable joint with an outer ligamentous membrane, an inner membrane producing lubricating synovial fluid, and cartilage over bone ends.
- **Agonist/antagonist**: two muscles acting on the same joint in opposite directions; the agonist contracts while the antagonist relaxes.
- **Liquid connective tissue**: blood; few cells in a liquid extracellular matrix (plasma).
- **Erythrocyte**: red blood cell, anucleate, organelle-free, filled with haemoglobin; lives ~3 months.
- **Leukocyte**: white blood cell; includes granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils) and agranulocytes (monocytes, lymphocytes).
- **Platelet**: small membrane-bound fragment of a megakaryocyte; participates in haemostasis.
- **Haemoglobin**: protein with four globin subunits, each containing a haem group with an iron atom; binds up to four O₂ molecules.
- **Haemostasis**: the set of mechanisms that stop bleeding: vascular spasm, platelet plug, coagulation.
- **Coagulation**: cascade conversion of soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin, forming a clot that traps blood cells.
- **Extrinsic pathway**: coagulation triggered when blood contacts tissue factor outside the vessel.
- **Intrinsic pathway**: coagulation triggered by platelet activation inside the blood.
- **Autorhythmic cells**: cardiac cells in a node that generate action potentials spontaneously at regular intervals.
- **Systole**: contraction of heart muscle; atrial systole precedes ventricular systole.
- **ECG**: recording of electrical activity of the heart from the body surface; P wave, QRS complex, T wave.

## Detailed notes

### 1. Skeletal muscle cell structure and contraction
[00:00:00–00:07:41]

- Each skeletal muscle cell has the usual plasma membrane, plus invaginations called **transverse (T) tubules** that let the membrane reach the cell interior.
- Inside the cell there is **endoplasmic reticulum** (sarcoplasmic reticulum) and **myofibrils**.
- Myofibrils are highly organised bundles of proteins forming functional units called **sarcomeres**.
- Each sarcomere contains **thick filaments** (central) and **thin filaments** (at the two sides), arranged as thin–thick–thin; many sarcomeres are attached in series along the long cell.
- Contraction means the whole muscle shortens and thickens; this requires each individual cell to contract.
- Skeletal muscle can only be activated by the arrival of **neurotransmitters** from a nerve terminal; each muscle cell needs one nerve terminal.
- Sequence of contraction:
  1. Neurotransmitter is released at the synapse onto the muscle cell.
  2. The muscle cell membrane undergoes an **action potential** (due to sodium entry and potassium exit).
  3. The action potential propagates along the membrane and into the T tubules.
  4. Because T tubules are close to the endoplasmic reticulum, this triggers **release of calcium** from the ER.
  5. Calcium helps thin filaments attach to thick filaments and move over them toward the centre of the sarcomere.
- As every sarcomere in the cell shortens simultaneously, the whole cell shortens.

### 2. Joints (articulations)
[00:07:41–00:15:24]

- Joints are the parts that keep adjacent bones in contact; most are formed by connective tissue.
- Three functional/structural classes:
  1. **Synarthrosis** – immobile; connective tissue/ligaments directly attach bones; e.g. bones of the cranium.
  2. **Amphiarthrosis** – allows small movement; has a cartilaginous disc; e.g. discs between adjacent vertebrae.
  3. **Synovial** – allows free movement; complex structure.
- Synovial joint structure:
  - Outer membrane is like a ligament.
  - Inner membrane contains cells that produce **synovial fluid**, filling the cavity and lubricating movement to avoid tissue damage.
  - A layer of **cartilage** covers the bone ends next to the fluid.
- Note: the functional and structural classifications usually match but not always perfectly.
- Many joints have **two muscles** associated, producing opposite movements.
  - Example: at the elbow, contraction of one muscle produces **flexion**; contraction of the other produces **extension**.
  - **Agonist** = the muscle that contracts to produce a given movement; **antagonist** = the muscle that relaxes.
  - In flexion, the flexor is agonist and extensor is antagonist; in extension, the roles reverse.
- Movement types: **angular** (reducing or increasing the angle between bones), **sliding**, **rotation**, and special movements; angular and rotation are most common in limbs. The most important joints for movement are associated with the shoulder girdle and pelvic girdle.

### 3. Cardiovascular system overview and blood components
[00:15:45–00:25:00]

- Components: **blood** (a liquid connective tissue), **heart**, and **vessels**.
- Blood is a connective tissue: few cells and a large extracellular matrix, here liquid plasma.
- Functions:
  - Transport of nutrients from intestines/lungs to tissues.
  - Transport of residues from tissues to removal sites (kidneys, lungs for CO₂).
  - Transport of heat, helping regulate body temperature.
  - Regulation of internal conditions: pH, water amount in cells, etc.
  - Protection: contains immune system cells; protects against blood loss via coagulation.
- Blood properties: temperature ~38°C, slightly basic pH, dense and viscous, represents ~8% of total body mass, total volume ~4–6 litres depending on the person.
- Two main parts:
  - **Liquid (plasma)**: mostly water, but ~7% proteins (albumin, globulins including antibodies, coagulation proteins), plus small molecules like glucose, cholesterol, urea, creatinine, ions.
  - **Corpuscular elements**: erythrocytes, leukocytes, and platelets.
- **Erythrocytes (red blood cells)** are the most abundant.
- **Leukocytes (white blood cells)** are a mixture:
  - Granulocytes: **neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils** (contain specific granules).
  - Agranulocytes: **monocytes** and **lymphocytes**.
- **Platelets** are not cells but **fragments of cells**, ~1 µm, fully membrane-enclosed.
  - Produced by fragmentation of **megakaryocytes** in red bone marrow.
  - Erythrocytes are produced from **reticulocytes** in red bone marrow; reticulocytes expel their nucleus and become erythrocytes, which therefore have **no nucleus and no organelles**.
- Some blood cells enter tissues for specific tasks:
  - Monocytes can become **macrophages**.
  - B lymphocytes can become antibody-producing cells in tissues.

### 4. Erythrocytes and haemoglobin
[00:25:00–00:29:35]

- Erythrocytes are small (7–8 µm diameter), biconcave discs, survive ~3 months, produced from reticulocytes after nuclear expulsion.
- They are essentially bags filled with **haemoglobin**.
- Haemoglobin structure:
  - Four protein subunits called **globins**.
  - Each globin contains a **haem group**, an organic molecule (not protein) with an **iron atom** at its centre.
  - Each iron binds one O₂, so one haemoglobin carries up to **four O₂ molecules**.
- After erythrocyte death, components are recycled:
  - Globin proteins → amino acids for reuse.
  - Haem group → **bilirubin**, excreted in urine or faeces, giving the typical colour of these residues; the haem group also gives blood its red colour.
  - Iron atoms are recycled and transported bound to **ferritin**, a blood protein.
- Because erythrocytes are numerous but short-lived, recycling is essential for producing new ones.

### 5. Haemostasis: stopping bleeding
[00:29:35–00:37:26]

- Blood must be kept inside vessels; if a vessel is damaged, mechanisms are needed to stop bleeding.
- **Three main mechanisms**:
  1. **Vascular spasm** – vasoconstriction at the injury site; muscular contraction reduces blood arriving to the lesion.
  2. **Platelet plug** – platelets aggregate and attach to each other, forming a plug that blocks the lesion.
  3. **Coagulation** – formation of a clot.
- Coagulation is different from the platelet plug:
  - It involves soluble proteins in plasma; among the 7% of plasma proteins, the key one is **fibrinogen**.
  - Fibrinogen is converted into **fibrin**, which aggregates and forms a **clot**.
  - Fibrin fibres trap anything moving nearby, including erythrocytes and platelets.
- Coagulation pathways are **cascades** of sequential steps, ending in fibrinogen → fibrin conversion.
  - **Extrinsic pathway**: initiated when blood contacts **tissue factor** from surrounding tissues after injury.
  - **Intrinsic pathway**: initiated by **platelet activation** from inside the blood.
- The platelet plug and coagulation are interconnected:
  - Platelet plug formation activates the intrinsic pathway, producing secondary clot.
  - Clot formation traps platelets, which become activated and help produce the platelet plug.
- There are also control mechanisms of anticoagulation to avoid unspecific clot formation.

### 6. Heart, circulation and the artery/vein distinction
[00:37:40–00:43:06]

- Blood moves because heart muscle cells contract and pump it through vessels.
- Blood reaches almost every body area, with exceptions: **epithelia** and **cartilage** are not irrigated.
- Heart anatomy: four compartments — two **atria** and two **ventricles**.
  - Veins bring blood to the atria.
  - Arteries carry blood away from the heart to the body.
- Two circuits:
  - **Pulmonary circulation**: heart → lungs → heart.
  - **Systemic circulation**: heart → all body tissues → heart.
- Colour coding: blood leaving the heart toward the lungs is **deoxygenated** (blue); blood leaving the heart toward tissues is **oxygenated** (red).
  - Blood returning from the body tissues is deoxygenated.
  - Blood returning from the lungs is oxygenated.
- Important distinction stressed by the professor:
  - **Artery** only means a vessel that leaves the heart.
  - **Vein** only means a vessel that returns blood to the heart.
  - Oxygen content is independent of this: the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood, and pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood.

### 7. Cardiac muscle, electrical activity and the ECG
[00:43:06–00:49:21]

- Cardiac muscle cells are **striated** like skeletal muscle but are **branched** and touch each other.
- Adjacent cardiac cells have **gap junctions** connecting cytoplasm to cytoplasm, allowing action potentials to propagate from one cell to its neighbours.
- Contraction mechanism is similar to skeletal muscle: action potential → calcium release from endoplasmic reticulum → sarcomere shortening.
- **Autorhythmic cells** in a specific **node** of the heart can spontaneously produce action potentials at regular intervals, without neural stimulation.
- Because all cardiac cells are connected by gap junctions, the impulse from the node spreads through the heart; this explains why the heart pumps regularly without neural input.
- Contraction is called **systole**. There are two contractions per pumping cycle: **atrial systole** first, then **ventricular systole** shortly after.
- This desynchronisation drives blood movement and is detectable externally.
- **Electrocardiogram (ECG)** records the heart’s electrical activity from the body surface:
  - **P wave**: electrical activation leading to atrial contraction.
  - **QRS complex**: activation of ventricular cells (action potential).
  - **T wave**: relaxation of the heart muscle cells.

### 8. The cardiac cycle: five steps
[00:49:21–00:52:37]

1. **Atrial contraction** – blood moves from atria into ventricles.
2. **Isovolumetric ventricular contraction** – ventricles begin to contract; the valves between atria and ventricles close because they allow flow in only one direction; volume stays constant while pressure rises until it is high enough to open the outflow valves.
3. **Ventricular ejection** – after enough pressure builds, the outflow valves open and blood is ejected from the ventricles.
4. **Isovolumetric relaxation** – ventricles begin to relax; all valves are closed; volume is constant while pressure falls.
5. **Ventricular filling** – when ventricular pressure drops enough, the atrioventricular valves reopen and blood flows into the ventricles, before a new cycle starts.

The professor asked students to review this five-step scheme and be able to connect muscle contraction, the electrical stimulus seen on the ECG, and blood movement.

## What the professor stressed
- **Do not confuse artery/vein with oxygenated/deoxygenated blood.** Artery means leaving the heart; vein means returning to the heart. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood; pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood. This was explicitly flagged as a common mistake.
- **The three haemostasis mechanisms are distinct but interconnected** — vascular spasm, platelet plug, and coagulation; platelet plug and clot formation activate each other bidirectionally.
- **The cardiac cycle five-step scheme is complex and exam-relevant**; the professor explicitly asked students to recheck and ensure they can explain how contraction, electrical activity (ECG), and blood movement are linked.
- **Skeletal muscle contraction absolutely requires a nerve terminal** for each muscle cell; activation cannot occur without neurotransmitter release.
- **Autorhythmic cells and gap junctions** explain how the heart pumps regularly without neural input — an important concept for understanding heart function.

## Self-test
1. Explain the sequence of events from nerve terminal activation to shortening of a skeletal muscle cell. Which role does calcium play?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Neurotransmitter is released from the nerve terminal and binds to the muscle cell, triggering an action potential in the membrane. The action potential propagates into the transverse tubules, which are close to the sarcoplasmic reticulum. This causes calcium release from the ER. Calcium promotes attachment of thin filaments to thick filaments and their sliding toward the centre of each sarcomere, shortening the sarcomeres and therefore the whole cell. Calcium is the key signal that links electrical excitation to filament movement.
</details>

2. Compare synarthrosis, amphiarthrosis and synovial joints in terms of movement and structure, with an example of each.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Synarthrosis: immobile; bones joined directly by connective tissue/ligaments; example: cranial bones. Amphiarthrosis: allows small movement; contains a cartilaginous disc; example: joints between adjacent vertebrae. Synovial: freely movable; has an outer ligamentous membrane, an inner membrane producing synovial fluid, and cartilage on bone ends; example: shoulder or elbow.
</details>

3. What are the main components and functions of blood?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Blood consists of plasma (water, ~7% proteins including albumin, globulins and coagulation factors, plus small solutes) and formed elements: erythrocytes, leukocytes and platelets. Functions: transport of nutrients, oxygen and waste; transport of heat; regulation of temperature, pH and water; protection via immune cells and prevention of blood loss through coagulation.
</details>

4. Why can an erythrocyte be described as “a bag of haemoglobin”, and how is its contents recycled after cell death?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Erythrocytes lose their nucleus and organelles, leaving a biconcave cell packed mostly with haemoglobin. Haemoglobin has four globin subunits, each with a haem group containing iron that binds one O₂, so each molecule carries up to four O₂. After death, globins are broken down into amino acids; haem is converted to bilirubin and excreted in urine/faeces (contributing colour); iron is recycled and transported by ferritin.
</details>

5. Distinguish the platelet plug and coagulation. How are the extrinsic and intrinsic coagulation pathways initiated?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The platelet plug is formed by platelets aggregating at the lesion site. Coagulation is a separate cascade that converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin, forming a clot that traps cells. The extrinsic pathway is triggered when blood contacts tissue factor from surrounding injured tissues. The intrinsic pathway is triggered by platelet activation inside the blood. The two mechanisms are interconnected: platelet plugs activate the intrinsic pathway, and clots trap and activate platelets.
</details>

6. A patient has blood in the pulmonary artery. Is it oxygenated or deoxygenated? Explain the principle behind your answer.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Deoxygenated. Artery means a vessel leaving the heart, regardless of oxygen content. The pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle to send blood to the lungs for oxygenation, so it carries deoxygenated blood. Similarly, pulmonary veins return oxygenated blood from the lungs to the heart.
</details>

7. How do cardiac muscle cells differ from skeletal muscle cells in terms of activation and impulse propagation?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Both are striated and use sarcomeres for contraction. However, cardiac muscle cells are branched and connected by gap junctions, so an action potential can propagate directly from one cell to adjacent cells. Additionally, the heartbeat is initiated by autorhythmic cells in a node that generate action potentials spontaneously; skeletal muscle requires nerve terminals to activate each cell.
</details>

8. What does each wave of the ECG represent, and how do they relate to the cardiac cycle?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
P wave: electrical activation leading to atrial contraction. QRS complex: activation/action potential of ventricular muscle cells. T wave: relaxation of heart muscle cells. These electrical events correspond to atrial systole, then ventricular systole, then relaxation of the heart.
</details>

9. Describe the five steps of the cardiac cycle, including when the atrioventricular valves are open or closed.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
1) Atrial contraction: AV valves open, blood moves from atria to ventricles. 2) Isovolumetric ventricular contraction: ventricles contract, AV valves close, volume constant, pressure rises. 3) Ventricular ejection: pressure opens outflow valves, blood is ejected. 4) Isovolumetric relaxation: ventricles relax, all valves closed, volume constant, pressure falls. 5) Ventricular filling: AV valves reopen and blood flows passively into ventricles before the next cycle.
</details>

10. Why do skeletal muscle cells need transverse tubules, and what would happen if calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum were blocked?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Transverse tubules carry the surface action potential deep into the cell, allowing the electrical signal to reach the sarcoplasmic reticulum and trigger calcium release. If calcium release were blocked, thin filaments would not be able to slide over thick filaments, so sarcomeres would not shorten and the muscle could not contract.
</details>
