## Summary
This session covered the digestive system, the urinary system, and the first part of the endocrine system. For the digestive system, the professor explained the organs involved, the common wall architecture of the digestive tract, its functions, the three phases of digestion, the secretions of the accessory glands, and the sites and mechanisms of absorption. For the urinary system, she explained the organs, the nephron as the functional unit, and the three processes of filtration, reabsorption and secretion. Finally, she introduced the endocrine system: the concept of a hormone, endocrine versus exocrine secretion, receptor types, and the hypophysis (pituitary) as the main link between the nervous and endocrine systems.

Key takeaways:
- Digestion combines enzymatic/chemical processing and mechanical processing (motility), not just enzymes.
- Absorption of most nutrients and water occurs mainly in the small intestine, especially via enterocytes with villi and microvilli.
- The nephron performs three processes: filtration in the renal corpuscle, reabsorption along the tubule, and secretion of additional substances into urine.
- Glomerular filtration is non-selective by size only; cells, platelets and most proteins are not filtered.
- Hormones are chemical signals released into interstitial fluid; water-soluble hormones need cell-surface receptors while lipid-soluble hormones can use intracellular receptors.
- The hypophysis integrates nervous and endocrine control: the neurohypophysis releases brain-produced hormones, while the adenohypophysis is controlled by hypothalamic releasing/inhibiting hormones via the bloodstream.

## Key concepts
- **Mucosa** — the innermost layer of the digestive tract wall, composed of epithelium, lamina propria and muscularis mucosae.
- **Submucosa** — connective tissue layer beneath the mucosa; can contain glands and ducts from accessory glands.
- **Muscularis externa (muscular layer)** — two smooth-muscle layers of the digestive tract: an inner circular layer and an outer longitudinal layer; drive motility.
- **Digestion** — the mechanical and chemical reduction of ingested food into small molecules that can be absorbed.
- **Cephalic, gastric, intestinal phases** — the three regulatory phases of digestion, triggered before food intake, when food reaches the stomach, and when food reaches the intestine.
- **Chylomicrons** — lipid-rich structures formed inside enterocytes after lipid absorption; they travel via the lymphatic system rather than directly into blood capillaries.
- **Nephron** — the functional unit of the kidney, consisting of the renal corpuscle and a tubular region.
- **Filtration** — non-selective, size-based movement of water and small solutes from glomerular blood into Bowman's capsule.
- **Reabsorption** — return of filtered substances from the renal tubule back to the blood.
- **Secretion** — additional movement of substances from the blood into the renal tubule for elimination in urine.
- **Hormone** — a chemical product released into the interstitial space that acts on target cells possessing the appropriate receptor.
- **Endocrine vs exocrine secretion** — endocrine secretion releases products into interstitial fluid without ducts; exocrine secretion releases products to the exterior (e.g., into the digestive tract) via ducts.
- **Neurohypophysis and adenohypophysis** — the two parts of the hypophysis: neurohypophysis releases hormones produced by hypothalamic neurons; adenohypophysis contains endocrine cells stimulated by hypothalamic releasing/inhibiting hormones.

## Detailed notes

### Digestive system: overview and organisation [00:00:00]
The digestive system includes the structures involved in food uptake and processing: the mouth and surrounding organs, the pharynx, the digestive tract (esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine), and accessory glands (salivary glands, liver, pancreas) [00:00:00–00:01:40].

All parts of the digestive tract — esophagus, stomach, small and large intestine — share a common anatomical organisation [00:01:40–00:02:46]:
- **Epithelium** at the border with the lumen; its exact type varies along the tract.
- **Lamina propria** beneath the epithelium (extracellular matrix with immune and support cells).
- **Muscularis mucosae**, a thin layer of smooth muscle.
- These three together form the **mucosa**.
- Below the mucosa: the **submucosa**, then the **muscularis**, organised into an inner circular layer and an outer longitudinal layer.
- Some regions have glands in the submucosa, and ducts carrying secretions from the accessory glands (liver, pancreas) open into the tract.

### Functions of the digestive system [00:02:46–00:04:33]
1. **Ingestion** — taking in food.
2. **Secretion** — release of liquids that mix with the ingested food.
3. **Motility** — muscular movement that mixes and moves contents along the tract.
4. **Digestion** — mechanical and chemical reduction of large particles/macromolecules into small soluble molecules.
5. **Absorption** — passage of small molecules into the blood.
6. **Elimination / defecation** — expulsion of non-absorbed contents.

### The three phases of digestion [00:05:45–00:06:50]
- **Cephalic phase** — occurs before food is even ingested; prepares mouth and stomach (e.g., secretion of saliva and gastric juice).
- **Gastric phase** — starts when food reaches the stomach; increased gastric motility and gastric juice secretion.
- **Intestinal phase** — starts when food reaches the intestine.

All phases use both **nervous** and **endocrine** regulation.

### Secretions into the digestive tract [00:08:44–00:11:57]
- **Saliva** (salivary glands): water, salts, **amylase** (degrades carbohydrates) and **lipases** (degrade lipids).
- **Stomach**: **lipases**, **pepsin** (a protease that cuts proteins into smaller pieces), and **hydrochloric acid (HCl)**. HCl denatures/unfolds proteins, making them more accessible to pepsin.
- **Pancreas**: amylases, lipases, trypsin (a protease), nucleases (degrade nucleic acids), and **bicarbonate** to neutralise the acidic content coming from the stomach.
- **Liver (bile)**: delivers cholesterol and **bile salts** to the intestine. Bile salts emulsify dietary lipids, aiding their digestion. Bile also carries **bilirubin**, a breakdown product of the heme group of erythrocytes; bilirubin is responsible for the colour of faeces and is the route by which heme waste is eliminated.
- **Small intestine**: secretes enzymes for the final enzymatic processing of macromolecules.
- **Mucus** is produced throughout and protects/lubricates the epithelium.

*Note (mine): the professor's summary table/scheme showed that about 9 litres of liquid per day enter the digestive tract (diet plus saliva, gastric juice, bile, pancreatic juice), most of which is absorbed in the small intestine; only a small fraction is lost in faeces [00:11:57].*

### Absorption sites [00:13:39–00:16:13]
- **Stomach** [00:13:39]: only small amounts of water and a few molecules are absorbed, including alcohol and some drugs. The stomach is not a major absorption site.
- **Small intestine** [00:14:47]: the main site of absorption for water and nutrients. The mucosa is folded into **villi**, and the enterocytes (absorptive epithelial cells) have further membrane folds (**microvilli**) on their lumenal surface, greatly increasing the absorptive surface.
- **Large intestine** [00:16:13]: fewer enterocytes and many more mucus-producing cells; some water and some substances are absorbed, but absorption is much less important here.

### Transport mechanisms in enterocytes [00:17:25–00:22:01]
- **Glucose and fructose** are taken up largely by **secondary active transport**. The cell uses ATP to generate an ion gradient (e.g., sodium). Sodium then moves into the cell down its gradient, and glucose is **co-transported** with sodium. It is called secondary because ATP is consumed in a different part of the cell to create the gradient, not directly to move the glucose [00:17:25].
- **Amino acids** are likewise taken up by sodium co-transport [00:18:56].
- **Lipids**: small lipids can cross directly through the membrane because of their size and lipid affinity. Larger lipids arrive at the enterocyte surface in **micelles** (emulsified structures), cross the membrane, and are then re-assembled inside the cell into **chylomicrons** — structures that allow hydrophobic lipids to be handled in an aqueous environment [00:18:56–00:20:25].
- Chylomicrons are **not** released directly into blood capillaries; they enter the interstitial space and are carried by the **lymphatic system**, later reaching the bloodstream [00:20:25–00:22:01].

### Fate of absorbed products [00:22:01]
Absorbed products entering the bloodstream do not go directly to the heart; they first reach the **liver**, which has a major role in metabolic control.

The professor stressed the general view: digestion means both **enzymatic processing of molecules** and **motility (mechanical processing)** — mastication in the mouth, strong mixing/fragmentation in the stomach, and movement along the whole tract. **Absorption occurs mainly in the small intestine** [00:23:32].

### Urinary system: organs and functions [00:23:32–00:25:06]
The urinary system consists of the **kidneys**, **ureters**, **bladder** and **urethra**. The kidneys produce urine, which is stored in the bladder and eliminated via the urethra.

Functions:
- Elimination of waste residues in urine.
- Regulation of blood composition.
- Regulation of pH (acidity of the internal medium).
- Regulation of fluid volume and arterial pressure (two interrelated characteristics).
- Regulation of osmolarity.
- Endocrine function: the kidneys produce hormones, including the one that regulates erythrocyte production (erythropoietin) [00:25:06].

The kidneys receive a very large blood flow: more than 1 litre of blood per minute arrives, and the final output is about **2 litres of urine per day** [00:25:06].

### The nephron [00:26:21–00:29:49]
The functional unit of the kidney is the **nephron**, composed of:
- **Renal corpuscle**: located in the kidney cortex (outermost part); the site where blood is filtered. It consists of the **glomerulus** (a network of capillaries) and the **Bowman's capsule** (the structure around the glomerulus that collects the filtered liquid).
- **Tubular region**, with segments in both cortex and medulla:
  - **Proximal convoluted tubule** (initial segment after the renal corpuscle).
  - **Loop of Henle** (middle segment).
  - **Distal convoluted tubule**, which drains into the **collecting duct**.
- One collecting duct receives liquid from more than one nephron.

### The three processes of the nephron [00:29:49–00:33:23]
1. **Filtration** — occurs in the renal corpuscle. Blood pressure drives the movement of water, ions and small molecules through the filtration membrane into Bowman's capsule.
2. **Reabsorption** — most of the filtered liquid is taken back. Most of the filtered water is reabsorbed; all glucose and amino acids are reabsorbed. Reabsorption is essential because filtration alone would produce far more than 2 L/day.
3. **Secretion** — additional components can be secreted into the urine. Examples: additional ions when they need to be eliminated, urea, and protons (important for pH control).

### Filtration in detail [00:30:53–00:35:19]
The filtration membrane lies between blood and the liquid in the renal corpuscle. It is formed by:
- The **endothelial cells** of the glomerular capillaries.
- A specialised cell type, the **podocytes**.
- The extracellular matrix / basal lamina between these two cell layers.

This membrane is **non-selective, selecting only by size**. Water, ions and small molecules (glucose, amino acids, bilirubin, urea) pass. Plasma proteins, blood cells, platelets and erythrocytes do **not** pass. The filtered liquid is held in the capsular space and directed into the renal tubule.

### Reabsorption along the tubule [00:35:19–00:38:01]
Reabsorption occurs along all segments of the tubule, but mostly in the **proximal convoluted tubule** (cortex). There:
- Most of the filtered water is reabsorbed.
- **All glucose and amino acids** are reabsorbed.
- Almost all filtered **bicarbonate** is reabsorbed.

In the **loop of Henle**, smaller percentages of water and ions are reabsorbed. In the **distal tubule**, fine regulation occurs, including fine control of water reabsorption. The **antidiuretic hormone (ADH)** acts here to determine how much water is retained or excreted.

Overall numbers [00:38:01]: about **180 litres of water filtered per day**, with **99% of the filtered water reabsorbed**. Glucose is completely reabsorbed in a healthy individual; proteins are almost not filtered at all.

### Secretion in detail [00:39:17–00:40:25]
- **Proximal tubule**: urea, ammonium, creatinine residues, and protons can be secreted. Proton secretion here helps in bicarbonate reabsorption.
- **Distal tubule and collecting duct**: protons are also secreted. Here the purpose is **blood pH regulation** — secreting protons into urine reduces protons in the bloodstream, helping to control body pH.

### Endocrine system: organisation [00:41:35–00:42:47]
The endocrine and nervous systems are the two main coordination systems of the body (with some participation of the immunological system). Endocrine components are organised into two categories:

1. **Endocrine glands** — organs whose unique function is to produce hormones: hypophysis, thyroid gland, parathyroid glands, suprarenal (adrenal) glands, pineal gland.
2. **Other endocrine organs** — organs that produce hormones in addition to other functions: e.g., testicles and ovaries (reproductive function plus hormone production), and most organs of the body.

### Hormone concept and receptors [00:44:24–00:48:34]
Cells communicate chemically. A hormone is different from gap-junction communication or synaptic communication: hormones are chemical products released into the **interstitial space**, from which they can diffuse and be detected by any cell with the appropriate receptor — a more general, non-specialised cell-to-cell signal.

Receptor location depends on the chemical nature of the hormone:
- **Water-soluble (hydrophilic) hormones** cannot cross the plasma membrane, so the target cell must have a **cell-surface receptor**.
- **Lipid-soluble (lipophilic) hormones** can cross the membrane, so the receptor can be **intracellular**.

Many hormones reach the bloodstream and act on tissues far from their production site; these are called **circulating hormones**. The endocrine system works especially with circulating hormones.

### Endocrine vs exocrine secretion [00:48:34–00:49:50]
- **Endocrine secretion**: the chemical compound is secreted into the interstitial fluid; no duct is needed.
- **Exocrine secretion**: the product is released to the exterior of the body (e.g., into the digestive tract, which is continuous with the external environment); exocrine glands need ducts. Endocrine glands never have ducts.

### Hormones from non-specialised organs [00:50:06]
Examples:
- Kidney: **erythropoietin**
- Stomach: **gastrin**, which stimulates gastric juice production
- Heart: a hormone that controls blood pressure and blood volume
- Many other organs also produce hormones; the list is very long.

### The hypophysis (pituitary) [00:51:20–00:55:57]
The hypophysis is an endocrine gland located beneath the brain and interconnected with it; it is the main point of interaction between the endocrine and nervous systems.

- **Neurohypophysis** [00:51:20–00:52:41]: hormones released here are produced by neurons whose cell bodies are in the brain. Their axons end close to the capillaries, releasing hormones directly into interstitial fluid (no synapse). The two hormones released are **oxytocin** and **antidiuretic hormone (ADH)**.
- **Adenohypophysis** [00:52:41–00:53:42]: contains non-neural endocrine cells that secrete hormones: **growth hormone (GH)**, **thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)**, **adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)**, and hormones related to the reproductive system. Different cell types produce different hormones.

**Control of the adenohypophysis** [00:53:42–00:55:57]: these cells secrete their hormones only when stimulated by other hormones produced in the **hypothalamus** (releasing or inhibiting hormones). The hypothalamic hormones do not reach the adenohypophysis by simple diffusion; they enter the bloodstream, travel the short distance through the blood, and are released at the adenohypophysis. So even over a very short distance, this is a circulating-hormone system.

This produces a hierarchical axis: **hypothalamus → hypophysis → third organ (e.g., thyroid, adrenal, gonads)**, controlling functions such as reproduction, metabolism and growth. ## Self-test
1. What are the six functions of the digestive system listed in the lecture?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Ingestion, secretion, motility, digestion, absorption, and elimination (defecation).</details>

2. A cross-section of the small intestine shows epithelium, lamina propria, muscularis mucosae, submucosa, and muscularis. Which three belong to the mucosa, and what is the orientation of the smooth muscle in the muscularis?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The mucosa consists of the epithelium, lamina propria and muscularis mucosae. The muscularis has an inner circular layer and an outer longitudinal layer of smooth muscle.</details>

3. The stomach secretes pepsin and HCl. Why is HCl important for protein digestion even though pepsin is the protease?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
HCl denatures/unfolds proteins, making them less folded and therefore more accessible to pepsin, which cuts them into smaller pieces.</details>

4. Glucose enters enterocytes by sodium co-transport. Explain why this is classified as secondary active transport rather than primary active transport.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
ATP is used by the cell to create a sodium ion gradient (usually at another part of the cell). Glucose then moves into the cell together with sodium down that gradient. The energy for glucose uptake comes indirectly from the ion gradient, not from ATP directly moving glucose, so it is secondary active transport.</details>

5. Why do chylomicrons not enter blood capillaries directly after lipid absorption in the small intestine?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Chylomicrons are large lipid-rich structures. They are released into the interstitial fluid and travel through the lymphatic system, which later delivers them to the bloodstream; they do not pass directly into the blood capillaries.</details>

6. List the three processes performed by the nephron and state where each one occurs.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Filtration occurs in the renal corpuscle (glomerulus + Bowman's capsule). Reabsorption occurs along the renal tubule, mainly in the proximal convoluted tubule. Secretion occurs in specific tubular segments, notably the proximal tubule and the distal tubule/collecting duct.</details>

7. The glomerular filtrate contains water, ions, glucose, amino acids, bilirubin and urea, but not plasma proteins, erythrocytes, leukocytes or platelets. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The filtration membrane is non-selective and selects only by size. It is formed by the glomerular capillary endothelial cells, podocytes, and the extracellular matrix between them. Only water and small molecules can cross; larger elements such as proteins and blood cells are retained in the blood.</details>

8. About 180 L of water are filtered per day but only about 2 L of urine are produced. Explain what accounts for this difference.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Most filtered water (about 99%) is reabsorbed along the renal tubule, mainly in the proximal convoluted tubule, with fine-tuning in the distal tubule under the influence of antidiuretic hormone. Therefore only a small fraction is excreted as urine.</details>

9. A healthy person excretes no glucose in urine. Why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
All filtered glucose is completely reabsorbed in the renal tubule, mainly in the proximal convoluted tubule, so none remains to be excreted in a healthy individual.</details>

10. Distinguish endocrine from exocrine secretion.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Endocrine secretion releases products (hormones) into the interstitial fluid, without any duct. Exocrine secretion releases products to the exterior of the body, e.g., into the digestive tract, and requires ducts.</details>

11. Cortisol is a lipid-soluble hormone; insulin is a water-soluble hormone. Where would you expect the receptor for each to be located, and why?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Cortisol is lipid-soluble, so it can cross the plasma membrane and its receptor can be intracellular. Insulin is water-soluble and cannot cross the membrane, so its receptor must be on the cell surface.</details>

12. The adenohypophysis is only a few millimetres from the hypothalamus, yet it is controlled by circulating hormones. Explain this.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Hypothalamic neurons produce releasing and inhibiting hormones that enter the bloodstream and travel the short distance to the adenohypophysis, where they leave the bloodstream and stimulate or inhibit the endocrine cells. Even over this very short distance, the signal uses the circulating-hormone pathway, not direct diffusion or synapses.</details>
