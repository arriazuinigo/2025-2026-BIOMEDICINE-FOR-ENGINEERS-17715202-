## Summary

This session completed the overview of membrane-bound organelles: the endoplasmic reticulum (rough and smooth), Golgi apparatus, endosomes, lysosomes and peroxisomes, and how they move material through the cell. The second half covered mitochondria in depth: its double-membrane structure, endosymbiotic origin, maternal inheritance, and central role in bioenergetics via the citric acid cycle and electron transport chain. The final part introduced cell functions, starting with cell division: the cell cycle phases, the need to duplicate cellular contents and organelles, and the checkpoints that control division.

- Rough ER synthesises plasma membrane proteins; smooth ER makes lipids and stores calcium.
- Vesicle traffic is bidirectional: ER → Golgi → cell surface, and plasma membrane → endosomes → lysosomes.
- Mitochondria originated by symbiosis, retain a 37-gene genome, and are inherited only from the mother.
- Cells cannot “burn” glucose in one step; mitochondria complete glucose oxidation and produce most ATP via the inner membrane.
- The cell cycle is divided into interphase (G1, S, G2) and M phase; non-dividing cells such as most neurons exit into G0.
- Cell division is controlled by checkpoints: the restriction point before S, a pre-M checkpoint for DNA replication, and the metaphase–anaphase transition.

## Key concepts

- **Endomembrane system**: the interconnected set of membrane-limited compartments (ER, Golgi, vesicles, endosomes, lysosomes) used to synthesise, process, transport and degrade cellular material.
- **Rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER)**: ER with attached ribosomes where plasma membrane proteins are synthesised.
- **Smooth endoplasmic reticulum (SER)**: ribosome-free ER involved in lipid synthesis, metabolic pathways and calcium storage.
- **Golgi apparatus**: organelle made of stacked disc-like sacs that receives proteins/lipids from the ER, continues their processing and dispatches them onward.
- **Endosome**: vesicle formed by endocytosis from the plasma membrane.
- **Lysosome**: degradative vesicle containing enzymes and low pH; it degrades extracellular material and worn-out cell components.
- **Peroxisome**: small vesicular compartment containing enzymes specialised in oxidation reactions.
- **Mitochondrion**: double-membrane organelle with its own genome; the main site of aerobic energy production.
- **Endosymbiosis**: origin of mitochondria by stable uptake of a prokaryotic organism into a pre-eukaryotic cell.
- **Mitochondrial inheritance**: maternal-only transmission of mitochondrial DNA through the oocyte.
- **Citric acid cycle / Krebs cycle**: mitochondrial matrix pathway that converts pyruvate to CO2 and generates energy carriers.
- **Electron transport chain**: inner mitochondrial membrane protein system that transfers electrons, pumps protons, and drives ATP synthase.
- **Cell cycle**: ordered sequence of growth, DNA replication and division: G1, S, G2, M.
- **Checkpoint**: control mechanism ensuring the cell only proceeds when conditions are adequate and previous steps are completed correctly.
- **Mitosis**: somatic/non-reproductive cell division producing two daughter cells genetically equal to the mother cell.

## Detailed notes

### Membrane organelles and intracellular trafficking

Membrane organelles communicate and transfer contents between different cell compartments by forming vesicles. Material can enter the cell by endocytic uptake into vesicles, or be exported by exocytosis. This allows the cell to move membranes, extracellular material, synthesised proteins and lipids from one compartment to another [00:01:12].

Trafficking is bidirectional. In one direction, proteins are synthesised in the ER, processed in the Golgi, and transported by vesicles to the plasma membrane. In the other direction, the plasma membrane forms endosomes that can fuse with lysosomes for degradation [00:12:42]. This means the cell can precisely control what is present on its surface: adding material when needed, and taking up/reducing surface components or extracellular material when needed [00:12:42].

### Endoplasmic reticulum

The endoplasmic reticulum is a network of interconnected membrane sacs connected to the nuclear envelope [00:01:12]. It is not uniform: some regions have ribosomes attached, others do not. This defines two functional types [00:02:33]:

- **Rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER)**: contains ribosomes. All proteins destined to be added to the plasma membrane are synthesised here [00:03:42].
- **Smooth endoplasmic reticulum (SER)**: lacks ribosomes. It performs lipid production and other metabolic pathways, and is important as a calcium reservoir. The professor gave the example of muscle cells: calcium stored in the smooth ER is needed for muscle contraction, which will be revisited when discussing skeletal and cardiac muscle [00:03:42–00:04:58].

### Golgi apparatus and the secretory pathway

The Golgi apparatus is not one single compartment but an organelle composed of organised stacks of disc-like sacs lying close together [00:05:13–00:06:28]. It receives proteins and lipids by vesicle trafficking from the ER, continues their processing—for example further lipid modifications or protein processing—and then passes them onward toward the plasma membrane [00:06:28].

The professor traced the path of a membrane protein: it is synthesised in the ER, removed in a vesicle, travels to the Golgi, moves from one Golgi sac to another by repeated vesicle formation and fusion, undergoes processing, and finally travels in a new vesicle to the cell surface, where it is released by exocytosis [00:07:29].

### Endosomes, lysosomes and peroxisomes

Endosomes are vesicles formed during endocytosis: the plasma membrane invaginates, pinches off and forms an internal vesicle [00:08:54]. Some endosomes contain material that must be degraded.

Lysosomes are vesicles that contain mainly degradative enzymes and extreme conditions such as low pH. When an endosome fuses with a lysosome, or becomes a lysosome, its contents are degraded [00:10:19]. This is important in cells specialised in taking up bacteria or other dangerous materials from outside the cell. Lysosomes also degrade internal cell components that are no longer functional: an old organelle can be encapsulated into a vesicle, fused with a lysosome and then degraded [00:10:19–00:11:30].

Peroxisomes are small vesicular membrane compartments containing enzymes specialised in oxidation reactions. They have a much more particular, oriented function than the large ER and Golgi organelles [00:11:30].

### Mitochondria: structure and origin

Mitochondria are membrane organelles with a distinctive double-membrane structure. Electron microscopy shows clear outer limits, but the organelle has two membranes [00:13:51–00:14:54]:

- The **outer membrane** is rounder.
- The **inner membrane** forms many invaginations and is much larger than the outer membrane.
- Between the two membranes is the **intermembrane space**.
- Inside the inner membrane is the **matrix** [00:14:54–00:16:16].

The two membranes have similar general composition—phospholipid bilayers with proteins—but their specific composition and proteins differ, giving them different functions. The inner membrane has more proteins and a larger surface area [00:16:16].

Mitochondria are the only organelle in animal cells with this double-membrane structure. The professor explained this by endosymbiosis: in advanced stages of evolution, a pre-eukaryotic cell with a nucleus took up a prokaryotic organism by endocytosis. The prokaryote stayed alive inside the larger cell, creating mutual dependence: the larger cell received useful materials produced by the mitochondrion, and the mitochondrion benefited from the protected environment. This was a “win-win” symbiosis selected by evolution [00:17:18–00:18:30].

### Mitochondrial genome and inheritance

Because mitochondria originated from an independent prokaryotic organism, they still contain their own genomic material. The mitochondrial genome contains **37 genes** required for the whole cell to be alive [00:18:30].

Mitochondrial DNA is inherited only through the **oocyte**, not through the spermatozoid. Therefore the female progenitor is responsible for transmitting mitochondria and their genes to the next generation [00:19:43].

Consequence: if a woman has a pathogenic mutation in a mitochondrial gene, all her progeny will inherit the same alteration. If the mitochondrial mutation is in the male progenitor, none of the progeny will inherit it, because spermatozoids do not contribute mitochondria to the embryo [00:19:43–00:20:49]. This pattern is called **mitochondrial inheritance**.

### Mitochondrial bioenergetics

The main role of mitochondria is bioenergetics: extracting as much energy as possible from chemical substances such as glucose [00:20:49].

If glucose is burned directly in oxygen, it releases a large amount of energy as it is converted to CO2 and water. However, this direct burning requires a very large activation energy, which is not possible inside a living cell. Cells therefore modify glucose slightly in many small steps. Each step has a much lower activation energy, and the total energy released is the same as direct burning [00:22:17–00:23:35].

Without mitochondria, the cell can only convert glucose to **pyruvate**, so it never extracts all the possible energy. With mitochondria, the final, largest part of the process can occur, because the complete oxidation of glucose takes place inside mitochondria [00:23:35–00:24:47].

Inside the mitochondrion:

1. **Pyruvate** enters the mitochondria and enters the **citric acid cycle** (Krebs cycle) in the matrix.
2. This multi-step chemical network converts pyruvate into **CO2** and generates energy.
3. The cell captures this energy as **ATP**, the molecule cells use to store and transfer energy for chemical reactions.
4. Pyruvate enters; **CO2, water and ATP** are produced. ATP can leave the mitochondria and be used anywhere in the cell [00:25:17–00:26:27].

The ATP-producing part occurs in the **inner mitochondrial membrane**, not in the matrix. The citric acid cycle occurs in the matrix, but the process that yields ATP is embedded in the inner membrane. This is why the inner membrane is so long and folded [00:27:34].

The mechanism is the **electron transport chain**. Proteins in the inner membrane transport electrons. This electron transport helps the proteins pump protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space, creating a proton gradient. Protons then flow back through **ATP synthase**, which produces ATP. The citric acid cycle in the matrix is coupled to electron transport and proton pumping in the membrane, converting pyruvate and oxygen into water, CO2 and ATP [00:27:34–00:30:33].

Because mitochondria are separate membrane-bound compartments, many transport steps are needed. Gases can cross membranes freely, but pyruvate and ATP require additional help to cross the mitochondrial membranes [00:30:33].

Finally, the professor placed the citric acid cycle in the centre of the cell’s metabolic map. The left side of the map represents **catabolism** (degradation of products to generate energy and smaller compounds); the right side represents **anabolism** (synthesis of new products). The citric acid cycle is the final step of catabolism and also the initial point for anabolism because it produces energy, so it plays a central role in cell metabolism [00:31:50–00:32:55].

The professor also noted the biomedical data relevance: measurements of protein alterations are increasingly important in neurodegeneration; metabolite analysis in blood is common; and genomic studies can include analysis of mitochondrial inheritance [00:33:56].

### Cell division: why duplication must precede division

Cells are able to copy themselves, and the information to do so is contained inside the cell. After division, the two resulting cells are equal in the information they contain [00:36:37–00:37:46].

Before division, a cell must duplicate its contents: the genomic material/chromosomes must be duplicated, and also other cell parts such as organelles. Most organelles **cannot be synthesised de novo**; they must be inherited from the mother cell [00:38:55–00:39:11].

After duplication, there must be a precise division, so each daughter cell receives exactly half of the materials. This is not random but a highly organised separation [00:39:11–00:39:40].

### Phases of the cell cycle

Cell division is structured into phases [00:39:40]:

- **S phase**: DNA synthesis; duplication of the genomic material. This is the central part of the cycle.
- **M phase**: segregation of the genomic material and division of the cell itself.
- **G1 phase**: growth phase before DNA synthesis.
- **G2 phase**: growth phase just before M phase.

G1, S and G2 together form **interphase**. The cycle can repeat: grow, duplicate DNA, grow more, divide into two, and start again [00:40:37–00:41:47].

Some cells exit the cell cycle and never divide again, for example **most neurons**. These cells are said to be in **G0** [00:41:47].

### Cell cycle checkpoints and mitosis

The cell cycle is precisely regulated and controlled. The professor described three checkpoints [00:42:52]:

1. **Restriction point / start checkpoint**: before S phase, before the cell commits to division. Once the cell passes this point and starts DNA duplication, it cannot go backwards. The cell requires a favourable environment and adequate conditions for proliferation.
2. **Pre-M checkpoint**: after DNA replication, before separation of the genomic material. Full completion of DNA replication must be confirmed to avoid errors in segregation.
3. **Metaphase-to-anaphase transition**: a checkpoint inside M phase [00:42:52–00:44:37].

For **somatic cell division**—division of any body cell that will not generate spermatozoids or oocytes, i.e. non-reproductive division—the process is **mitosis**. During mitosis, DNA is duplicated so chromosomes contain two copies of the genomic material; these are then segregated so each daughter cell receives one copy, making the daughter cells exactly equal to the mother cell [00:44:37–00:45:40].

## What the professor stressed

- Mitochondria are “really, really relevant” for understanding bioenergetics; complete glucose oxidation occurs only inside mitochondria [00:24:47].
- The inner mitochondrial membrane is larger because that is where ATP is produced; its folded structure is not incidental [00:27:34].
- Most organelles are not made from scratch; they must be inherited from the mother cell before division [00:38:55].
- Mitochondrial inheritance is strictly maternal: a mother with a mitochondrial mutation passes it to all children; a father with such a mutation passes it to none [00:19:43–00:20:49].
- The citric acid cycle occupies a central position in metabolism: it is the final step of catabolism and the starting point for anabolism [00:31:50–00:32:55].
- The cell cycle has critical checkpoints; the restriction point before S is a point of no return [00:42:52–00:44:00].
- For health data science, mitochondrial genome data, metabolite measurements and protein alteration measurements are relevant biomedical data sources [00:33:56].

## Self-test

1. Why can the same endoplasmic reticulum contain both rough and smooth regions, and what functional difference does this create?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
The ER is a continuous membrane network, but ribosomes are attached only in some regions. Regions with ribosomes are rough ER and synthesise plasma membrane proteins. Regions without ribosomes are smooth ER and carry out lipid synthesis, other metabolic pathways and calcium storage.
</details>

2. Trace the route of a newly synthesised plasma membrane protein from its synthesis to the cell surface.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
It is synthesised on the rough ER, packaged into a vesicle, travels to the Golgi apparatus, moves through Golgi sacs by vesicle formation and fusion while being processed, then travels in a vesicle to the plasma membrane and is released by exocytosis.
</details>

3. Why do lysosomes need both degradative enzymes and low pH?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
The enzymes break down macromolecules, and the low pH provides the extreme acidic conditions required for their optimal activity. This allows lysosomes to degrade bacteria taken up from outside and old cell components.
</details>

4. How is the structure of the inner mitochondrial membrane related to its role in ATP production?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
The inner membrane is much larger than the outer membrane and folded into many invaginations. The electron transport chain and ATP synthase are located there, so more membrane surface allows more protein complexes and more ATP production.
</details>

5. A man has a disease caused by a mitochondrial gene mutation. Explain why his children are unaffected, but if his wife had the same mutation all their children would be affected.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Mitochondria are inherited only through the oocyte. Spermatozoids do not contribute mitochondria to the embryo, so a father’s mitochondrial mutation is not passed on. The mother’s mitochondrial DNA is inherited by all children, so her mutation would be transmitted to all of them.
</details>

6. Why can’t cells obtain energy from glucose by simply “burning” it in one reaction with oxygen?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Direct burning releases a lot of energy but requires a very high activation energy that is not compatible with living cells. Cells instead modify glucose in many small steps, each with a low activation energy, releasing the same total energy in a controlled way.
</details>

7. Where do the citric acid cycle and the electron transport chain take place, and what does each accomplish?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
The citric acid cycle occurs in the mitochondrial matrix and converts pyruvate into CO2. The electron transport chain occurs in the inner membrane, transports electrons, pumps protons into the intermembrane space, and the resulting proton gradient drives ATP synthase to produce ATP.
</details>

8. Why is the citric acid cycle described as the central point between catabolism and anabolism?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
It is the final step of catabolism, where degradation products converge, and it produces energy that feeds anabolism, the synthesis of new cell products. Thus it connects the two sides of the metabolic network.
</details>

9. Why must organelles be duplicated or inherited before cell division, rather than synthesised from scratch afterward?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Most organelles cannot be synthesised de novo by a cell. Each daughter cell must inherit organelles from the mother cell, so the contents must be duplicated before division and then shared precisely between the two daughter cells.
</details>

10. List the phases of the cell cycle and explain what happens in each. Where does G0 fit?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
G1: growth before DNA synthesis. S: DNA synthesis/duplication of genomic material. G2: growth and preparation before division. M: segregation of chromosomes and cell division. G1, S and G2 form interphase. G0 is a non-dividing state that some cells, for example most neurons, enter after leaving the cycle.
</details>

11. What are the three checkpoints mentioned in the session, and what does each ensure?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
1. The restriction point before S ensures conditions are favourable and the cell is committed to division. 2. The pre-M checkpoint ensures DNA replication is fully completed before chromosome segregation. 3. The metaphase-to-anaphase checkpoint controls correct progression within M phase.
</details>

12. What distinguishes somatic cell division (mitosis) from reproductive division as introduced in the session?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Somatic cell division/mitosis occurs in body cells that will not generate spermatozoids or oocytes. It produces two daughter cells that are not transmitted to the next generation and are genetically equal to the mother cell, each with one copy of the duplicated DNA.
</details>
